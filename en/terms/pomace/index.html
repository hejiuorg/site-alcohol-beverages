Pomace

Pomace is the solid residue of skins, pulp, seeds and stems left after pressing grapes, distinct from lees; it can be distilled into grappa or marc and used to recover tartaric acid, but must be handled quickly to avoid acetification.

Pomace (French marc) is the solid part left after grapes are pressed: skins, pulp residue, seeds and sometimes stems. It is a by-product of winemaking, but not waste; in Italy, France, Spain and the Balkans it is fermented and distilled into spirits with local identities. To understand pomace, look at its composition, moisture, microbial risk and legal identity together.

Definition and boundaries

Pomace differs from lees: lees are the yeast and fine particles that settle after fermentation, while pomace is the solid remains of the fruit itself. It also differs from stillage or vinasse, which usually means the residual liquid after distillation. White pomace is pressed before alcoholic fermentation, stays high in sugar and spoils easily; red pomace is separated after fermentation, soaked with wine, dark and high in moisture. The three are different objects in both process and regulation and should not be covered by one vague word. Moisture depends on press type and pressure: basket presses, pneumatic membrane presses and continuous presses leave pomace that differs in porosity, residual sugar and distilling behaviour.

Etymology and origin

Pomace comes from Middle English pomace, related to the pomace of apples, and later came to mean pressed fruit residue; French marc means press residue and also names spirits such as Marc de Bourgogne. Italian grappa grew out of the tradition of distilling grape pomace and is now a protected geographical indication; Spain and Portugal use orujo, and the Balkans have names such as rakija. These names are not just language differences; they correspond to different raw-material rules and geographical indications.

Mechanism and parameters

Press strength shapes the pomace: harder pressing leaves less wine but can bring coarser tannin and bitterness; gentle pressing leaves it wetter and more prone to fermentation. Moisture, residual sugar, acidity and microbial load set the handling route. Pomace is rich in tartrates that can be recovered as tartaric acid, and it contains skin pigments and phenols that affect the colour and astringency of a distillate. Higher temperature and longer storage make acetic acid bacteria and moulds more active. Residual sulfur dioxide in the pomace suppresses microbes but can bring a sharp sulfur note during distillation, so "sulfiting to preserve" and "distillate flavour" must be traded off. Freshness is therefore a process parameter, not just a hygiene slogan.

Practical use in the industry

The first use is distillation. Pomace can be fermented and then distilled: grappa in Italy, marc in France, orujo in Spain and Portugal, rakija and similar names in the Balkans. EU rules list grape marc spirit as a spirit-drink category with a minimum alcoholic strength of 37.5% vol. The second use is tartaric acid recovery for food and chemical applications. The third is compost and feed, with attention to residual sulfur dioxide and pesticides. The fourth is reprocessing, where a few practices re-ferment pomace with water or sugar, but such products usually cannot be labelled as wine. Grappa's legal identity is tied to grape pomace, and French marc is often named by origin; the same raw material can fall under different geographical indications and alcohol rules in different countries. For a winery, pomace handling is both a cost and a possible by-product income stream.

Confusable terms

First, pomace versus lees. Pomace is pressed solid; lees is fermentation sediment. Second, pomace versus must. Must is the juice and solids mixture from pressing, while pomace is the residue after juice is taken. Third, grappa versus pomace brandy. Grappa is an Italian protected name, not a generic term for all pomace spirits; French marc and Spanish orujo each have their own legal identity. Fourth, pomace versus grape skin. Skins are one component of pomace, which also contains pulp residue, seeds and sometimes stems; equating pomace with skins understates its complexity.

Common misuses and disputes

First, equating pomace with lees; second, calling pomace brandy "distilled wine" when its raw material is solid residue; third, treating grappa as a generic name; fourth, assuming pomace has no value. There is dispute over how quickly pomace should be distilled: tradition stresses fresh handling to keep fruit aromas, while modern practice also uses cold storage, ensiling and sulfiting, though sulfur affects later distillate flavour and regulatory compliance. Another dispute concerns press strength, since extracting more wine and preserving pomace quality often require a trade-off. Another misuse is to treat pomace brandy as a synonym for cheap liquor; grappa and marc also include long-aged, single-origin, high-priced products, and quality depends on raw-material handling and distillation precision.

Typical figures and examples

EU rules require grape marc spirit to have a minimum alcoholic strength of 37.5% vol; grappa, as an Italian geographical indication, is also made from grape pomace. With white pomace, residual sugar remains high after pressing, and if it is piled in a warm place for hours to days, acetic acid bacteria turn residual alcohol into acetic acid and produce a vinegar smell. Red pomace is dark and wet; if it soaks too long before distillation, the spirit can carry colour and more astringency. Freshly distilled white pomace spirit is usually colourless and only turns amber after wood ageing. These changes show that colour and flavour come more from handling than from the name of the raw material. Tartrates concentrate in pomace and can form potassium bitartrate crystals on cooling; industry recovers tartaric acid from pomace, a concrete reason why pomace is not waste.

Recording and describing

When recording a pomace product, note the raw material (white or red pomace), whether it was fermented, the distillation method, whether it was wood-aged and the alcohol content. In tasting, separate fruit, floral, herbal, bitter and astringent notes; colour is not the only clue to raw material because wood ageing changes it. When buying, grappa, marc and orujo names are bound by regional rules, so the name alone cannot establish raw material or process; check the producer and origin. Read the maceration and distillation records together to reconstruct where the pomace came from and how it was handled; if only "grappa" is known without raw material and distillery, the information is still incomplete.
