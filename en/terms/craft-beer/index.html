Craft beer

A small-scale, independent and tradition-minded beer production and consumption movement that emerged in the United States in the late 20th century; an industry and market concept, not a legal grade or a guarantee of quality.

Craft beer is a beer production and consumption movement that emerged in the United States in the late 20th century, centred on small, independent breweries that value tradition as well as experiment. It describes scale, ownership and process orientation. It is not a legal grade, not a single style, and not a promise of quality.

Figure: Craft beer. Image: Bernt Rostad, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Definition and boundaries

The Brewers Association defines a craft brewer through three conditions: small, independent and traditional. Small means annual production of no more than 6 million barrels, roughly 3% of US annual sales. Independent means less than 25% of the brewery is owned or controlled by a beverage alcohol industry member that is not itself a craft brewer. Traditional means most volume comes from beer with a malt base, brewed with traditional or innovative ingredients and fermentation. The thresholds are adjusted over time and belong to an industry definition, not the law.

Craft is therefore not a quality label that any product may attach to itself. Usage differs between countries, and in the Chinese market the word is mostly a marketing and category term. Judging a beer still means checking raw materials, process, origin and label information. Treating craft as a legal grade is the first misconception to remove.

Origin and industry context

Craft beer is an English compound: craft refers to skill or handiwork, beer to the drink. In the 1980s, US media and industry began using the phrase to distinguish small-brewery products from mass-market lager. The earlier word microbrewery stressed size; craft beer bundles scale, ownership and process orientation into a broader idea.

The phrase's rise is tied to structural change in the US market. After the Second World War the market was highly concentrated and dominated by light lager. In 1978 home brewing was permitted at the federal level, giving enthusiasts room to experiment, and small breweries followed. In 1980 Sierra Nevada was founded in California and later reached the market with a hop-forward pale ale, becoming a landmark of the movement. The older Anchor Brewing, founded in 1896, is seen as a pioneer that revived traditional styles.

Three hard criteria: small, independent, traditional

Small is a volume test. The six million barrel figure is not eternal; the association adjusts it as the market changes, so applying today's threshold to a brewery ten years ago can give a different answer. Independent is an ownership test. The 25 percent line concerns ownership or control by a non-craft beverage alcohol company, not by an ordinary investment fund. Traditional is the most misread criterion: it does not require a brewery to brew only old styles, but requires most volume to come from malt-based beer while allowing innovative ingredients and fermentation.

Together the three conditions describe an industry identity, not a taste profile. A brewery can lose independent status through acquisition, or outgrow the small threshold; its beer may not become better or worse, but its label identity changes. Understanding this is necessary for reading industry statistics and market reports.

How the term is used in the industry

In the United States, craft is used for market segmentation, industry statistics and consumer communication. The association publishes production, brewery-count and market-share data, media use the word for independent breweries, and retailers use it as a shelf category. It is not a legal beer category and does not set tax rates or labelling requirements.

In China, craft is mostly a category and marketing word. Consumers use it for beers that are flavourful, hop-forward and higher priced; producers use it to distinguish their products from industrial lager. Because there is no single legal definition, the same beer may carry different labels in different channels. For readers, the practical route is to check ingredients, alcohol content, bitterness, origin and production date rather than the word alone.

Styles and hops: not one beer

American craft beer is known for hop expression. Cascade, Citra and Mosaic bring citrus, pine, tropical fruit and berry aromas. West Coast IPA stresses bitterness and a dry finish; hazy IPA stresses a cloudy appearance and softer bitterness; sour, barrel-aged and fruit beers also developed within this system. The BJCP style guidelines give descriptive boundaries for these types, but a style guide is a judging tool, not a legal classification.

Barley remains the base of most products, while wheat, oats, rye and various adjuncts are widely used. Style innovation does not excuse weak fundamentals: mashing, fermentation and hygiene still decide success. Equating craft with IPA ignores saison, sour beer, porter and wheat beer, and treating "more hops" as a standard hides questions of balance and process.

Contrast with English real ale and Czech Pilsner

English ale is marked by pub culture, cask real ale and malt-hop balance. CAMRA, founded in 1971, brought real ale, unfiltered and still fermenting in the cask, to public attention. Czech Pilsner is marked by soft water, Saaz hops and long cold lagering. American craft beer treats both traditions as references while putting greater emphasis on hop expression and stylistic experiment.

The three are not substitutes but different industry and consumption paths. Treating craft beer as an upgraded version of traditional beer ignores its distinctive background in scale, ownership and market structure. English real ale stresses freshness and low carbonation, while American craft stresses cold-chain distribution and bottled or canned packaging; their supply chains follow different logic.

Terms easily confused

The first pair is craft beer and microbrewery. Microbrewery stresses small size; craft beer also includes independent ownership and process orientation. A microbrewery may not be recognised as craft by the trade body, and a larger regional brewery may still qualify. The second pair is craft beer and handmade beer. "Handmade" stresses manual work, but modern craft breweries use automated equipment too; craft is about scale, independence and process choices, not a rejection of machines.

The third pair is craft beer and IPA. IPA is a style; craft is an industry identity. The craft movement popularised IPA, but craft does not equal IPA. The fourth pair is craft and "good beer". That is a value judgement, not a definition. Small batches can bring more experiment and also more faults. Beer quality depends on raw materials, fermentation control, hygiene and freshness.

Common misuses and disputes

First, treating craft as a legal grade. It is an industry and market concept. Second, assuming craft always tastes better. Small batch does not equal high quality. Third, equating craft with IPA. Fourth, assuming craft never uses adjuncts; adjunct use depends on recipe and regulation. Fifth, applying the American definition directly to other countries, where usage and thresholds differ.

The main disputes concern independence and scale. When a large group buys a small brewery, industry bodies and consumers often disagree on whether the product may still be called craft; a brewery that outgrows the volume threshold may be removed from statistics, feeding the argument that craft is only a marketing device. Another dispute is the broadening of the word in China: when it is used for every expensive beer, consumers cannot use it to judge process or quality. For the United States, these arguments are a by-product of a mature market; for other markets, they are a warning against treating one country's concept as a universal standard.

Typical figures and examples

Verifiable figures include: the US craft brewery volume threshold of 6 million barrels, roughly 3 percent of US annual sales; less than 25 percent ownership or control by a non-craft beverage alcohol company; federal permission for home brewing in 1978; the founding of Sierra Nevada in 1980; the founding of Anchor Brewing in 1896; and the founding of CAMRA in 1971.

Concrete examples: a West Coast IPA may be 6.5%-7.5% alcohol by volume with 50-70 IBU; a hazy IPA may have similar alcohol but lower bitterness and a cloudier appearance; a Belgian saison is often 5%-8% alcohol by volume with spice and fruit character. These figures illustrate style differences; they are not a quality ranking.
