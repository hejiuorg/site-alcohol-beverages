Blend

A blend combines different varieties, vintages, sites or base wines into a final product to balance structure, stabilise style or meet regulations; it is a normal craft, not a synonym for low quality or adulteration.

A blend combines different varieties, vintages, sites or base wines into a final product. It is one of the most common operations in drinks production, not a synonym for adulteration or inferior goods. Wine, sparkling wine and whisky all have mature blending traditions; what differs is what is combined and why.

Definition and boundaries

The boundary is that the combination creates a new whole, rather than simply pouring two bottles together. Varietal blending changes acidity, tannin, aroma and body; vintage blending changes ripeness and stability; site blending changes terroir expression; base-wine blending changes style and supply scale. Every combination needs proportion design and repeated tasting, and the result is a new formula rather than a mechanical sum of ingredients.

A blend is also not “without origin”. Many blends come from a clear region and simply do not name a single variety; others cross regions or distilleries, as blended Scotch does. To judge how complete a blend's information is, look for variety, vintage, region and base-wine source, not just the word “blend”.

Etymology and origin

“Blend” comes from Old English and Old Norse words for mixing, and later entered the wine and spirits trade for combining components into a unified product. French uses assemblage for wine blending and cuvée for a batch of base wine or the blended liquid; the English word is broader and can cover combinations of variety, vintage, site or distillery.

Blending is older than single-varietal labelling. Before modern variety classification and regulation, growers often picked and fermented several varieties from the same plot together, which is both a field blend and one of the earliest forms of blending. With variety identification, appellation rules and global trade, blending split into the modern method of fermenting separately and then combining by proportion, and the traditional method of mixed planting and co-fermentation.

Mechanism and parameters

The core parameters are proportion, the state of the base wines and the target style. Varietal blending often works by complementarity: one variety supplies structure and tannin, another supplies soft fruit and acidity; for example, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and others build the body of a red wine. Proportions are not fixed; the winemaker adjusts them to the year so the final wine reaches the intended balance.

Champagne blending is more complex. Trade descriptions explain that houses combine base wines from different growths, varieties and vintages to create a consistent house style; non-vintage Champagne typically also uses reserve wines, while vintage Champagne relies mainly on base wines from a single year. Blending requires repeated tasting and comparison before the final mix is homogenised in large tanks. Here “blend” is both technique and style management.

Practical use in the drinks industry

In wine, blending is common in Bordeaux-style reds, the Rhône, Champagne and many New World wines. A Bordeaux red blend often combines Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc, Petit Verdot and Malbec to balance structure, aroma and ageing potential. In sparkling wine, non-vintage blending seeks consistency and vintage blending emphasises a single year.

In whisky, the regulations divide blended products into categories: blended Scotch is a blend of one or more single malt Scotch whiskies with one or more single grain Scotch whiskies; blended malt is a blend of single malts from different distilleries; blended grain is a blend of single grains from different distilleries. All are blends, but their legal compositions differ, and they should not be lumped together as “blended whisky”. Correct label reading depends on knowing these categories.

Related terms compared

The first pair is blend and single varietal. A blend stresses combination and balance, while a single varietal stresses recognition of one dominant variety; a blend can still be dominated by one variety, but the label usually will not name only that variety. The second pair is blend and field blend. A field blend is planted, harvested and fermented together; a conventional blend is often fermented separately and combined later. Field blend is “mixed in the field”, conventional blend is “mixed in the winery”.

The third pair is blend and “adulteration”. In spirits language, adulteration often implies adding water, flavour or colour and carries a negative connotation; blending is the combination of base spirits and does not imply fraud. The fourth pair is blended Scotch and blended malt. The first contains grain whisky, the second only single malts from different distilleries; both are blends, but their legal compositions differ. The fifth pair is blend and “low quality”; blending is a production route, not a quality grade.

Common misuses and debates

First, equating blending with adulteration. Second, confusing blend with blended malt and blended Scotch. Third, assuming a blend has no origin information. Fourth, ignoring how blend proportions affect style. Fifth, treating a non-vintage blend as a defect of “having no vintage”.

Debate centres on label transparency. Buyers want to know the proportions of varieties, vintages or distilleries, but most labels name only the main components and omit the full recipe; some appellation rules allow minor varieties to remain undisclosed within a set share. Another debate is the romanticising of field blends: they can bring integration, but they are also limited by uneven variety proportions and ripening, and not every mixed planting makes a good wine.

Typical figures and examples

The EU varietal labelling rule gives a checkable figure: one named variety must be at least 85%, while two or more named varieties must together make up 100%. This directly shapes blend labels: a wine with 70% Cabernet Sauvignon and 30% Merlot may show both varieties with their proportions, but may not name only “Cabernet Sauvignon”.

Champagne offers a clearer case: non-vintage Champagne may combine base wines from different years with reserve wines, while vintage Champagne relies mainly on base wines from one year; the house repeatedly tastes combinations of growths, varieties and vintages to maintain a stable brand style. For whisky, blended Scotch combines single malt and single grain, while blended malt combines single malts from different distilleries. Comparing these categories with the stated proportions prevents “blend” from becoming a vague catch-all.
