Dry

Dry is a residual-sugar category on wine labels, usually no more than 4 g/L under EU rules and up to 9 g/L when acidity is high; it says nothing about fruit intensity or quality.

Dry is the word wine labels use for a very low level of sugar left after fermentation. It answers a narrow question: how much sugar remains? It does not say how intense, good or fruity the wine is. Reading dry as "no fruit" or "top quality" is one of the most common mistakes made at the shelf.

Definition and boundaries

Under EU wine labelling rules, dry normally means residual sugar of no more than 4 g/L. When total acidity, expressed as tartaric acid, is at least 7 g/L, the ceiling may rise to 9 g/L. The relaxation is not a loophole; it acknowledges that acidity suppresses sweetness. The same 8 g/L of sugar may be almost invisible in a high-acid wine. Above that band, the wine moves into medium dry, medium sweet or sweet.

The boundary is a regulatory threshold, not "zero sugar". The timing of fermentation arrest, the yeast, filtration and any sweetening all change the final figure. A wine labelled dry may still contain 2-3 g/L of glucose and fructose, which is normal. What matters is the rule that applies to the product and the analytical basis, not a guess from taste.

Residual sugar is the core variable. For white wine, dry usually means a clear acid line and direct fruit; for red wine, dry may also come with the grip of tannin. Both are called dry, but the sensation comes from different places.

Where the word came from

English dry descends from Old English dryge, meaning free of moisture. From the 17th century onward, English wine writing used dry for wine without sweetness, and the word later entered labels and tasting notes. The Chinese term for dry is a translation rather than a literal transfer of "desiccated". Because the Chinese character can suggest astringency, it is easily confused with tannin.

French uses sec, German trocken and Spanish seco, all pointing to dryness. Each labelling system evolved separately, so thresholds differ across borders. Reading the label of the country of origin is usually more reliable than reading a translation.

The acidity mechanism behind the threshold

Sweetness is not a function of sugar alone. Acidity and sweetness suppress one another: high acid makes a wine seem fresher and drier, while low acid lets a small amount of sugar stand out. The EU condition of 7 g/L total acidity writes that sensory relationship into law. Total acidity and pH are different measures. The first is the titrated acid load, the second the concentration of hydrogen ions. A wine with a high pH does not necessarily have low total acidity, so pH alone cannot settle the question.

Temperature also matters. Cool temperatures suppress sweetness and sharpen acidity, so the same bottle may seem drier at 8 °C than at 18 °C. Alcohol and glycerol work the other way, adding roundness and a faint impression of sweetness. Separating these variables explains why "dry" and "tastes sweet" are not opposites.

How the term is used on labels

EU sweetness terms are tied to residual-sugar bands, and producers may use them only when the conditions are met. China's GB/T 15037-2006 Wines also divides wines by total sugar: dry is no more than 4.0 g/L, and the ceiling may reach 9.0 g/L when the difference between total sugar and total acidity is no more than 2.0 g/L. Note that the Chinese standard uses total sugar, not residual sugar in the strict sense, and the two are not identical for analysis or conversion.

United States federal labelling rules are looser on sweetness terms for still wine. "Dry" appears mostly in marketing and tasting notes rather than being defined by a sugar-plus-acidity formula as in the EU. When comparing markets, confirm the standard before comparing numbers, and do not transfer one country's "dry" to another country's product. A wine label may also carry alcohol, origin and variety, which sit alongside sweetness rather than replacing it.

Sparkling wine: a separate scale

Sparkling wine uses its own sweetness words: brut, extra dry, sec, demi-sec and doux. Their sugar comes from the dosage added after disgorgement, not from sugar left in the base wine. Brut is usually no more than 12 g/L; extra dry about 12-17 g/L; sec about 17-32 g/L; demi-sec about 32-50 g/L; doux above 50 g/L. Figures vary slightly with regulation and product, but the order is stable.

The common trap is assuming extra dry is drier than brut. In fact extra dry contains more sugar than brut; the name is a historical accident. Chinese translations that render brut as "dry" and extra dry as "super dry" make the confusion worse. Remember the bands rather than the translated names.

Terms easily confused

The first pair is dry and tannic or astringent. Dry describes sugar; tannic describes the grip and roughness of tannin. A red wine can be dry and astringent, or dry without being astringent. Calling astringency "dry" makes tasting notes impossible to compare.

The second group is dry, semi-dry and sweet. They are neighbouring bands in one labelling system, with boundaries set by sugar and acidity. Semi-dry sits above dry, and sweet occupies the higher sugar range. They are sugar categories, not levels of flavour intensity.

The third pair is dry in still wine and brut in sparkling wine. The first classifies residual sugar in a still wine or base wine; the second describes final sweetness after dosage. The two vocabularies cannot be swapped.

Common misuses and disputes

The most common misuse is treating dry as "no fruit". Dry only says sugar is low; fruit comes from variety, ripeness, fermentation and ageing and can be intense. Equally wrong is treating dry as a quality grade: it is a category, not a score. Calling the roughness of red wine "dry" mixes two sensory dimensions.

Two disputes persist. One is whether thresholds should be tied to acidity: supporters say the rule tracks perception, critics say it is too complex for consumers. The other is whether countries and varieties should share one sweetness vocabulary; at present there is no global definition, so comparisons must return to the applicable regulation. A further point is often missed: the pairing value of dry wine comes from acidity and structure, not from the absence of sugar. With dessert, a dry wine is often more likely to seem tart than a semi-dry one.

Typical figures and examples

Under EU rules: dry is up to 4 g/L, or up to 9 g/L when total acidity is at least 7 g/L; medium dry is above 4 and up to 12 g/L, with a ceiling of 18 g/L when total acidity is at least 10 g/L; medium sweet is above 12 and up to 45 g/L; sweet is above 45 g/L. For sparkling wine the usual bands are brut up to 12 g/L, extra dry 12-17 g/L, sec 17-32 g/L, demi-sec 32-50 g/L and doux above 50 g/L.

Two everyday examples. A dry Alsace white may carry only 2 g/L of sugar, yet ripe fruit makes it feel round. A brut Champagne with about 10 g/L of sugar feels crisp because of high acidity and bubbles. In the other direction, a low-acid, high-alcohol dry wine can seem sweet. Numbers and perception do not always line up.
