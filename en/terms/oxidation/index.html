Oxidation

Oxidation is the reaction of compounds in wine with oxygen, altering aroma, colour and tannin structure; a small controlled amount builds styles such as Oloroso, Madeira and vin jaune, while excess brings loss of fruit, browning and acetaldehyde.

Oxidation is the reaction of compounds in wine with oxygen. It is not a single reaction but a chain: ethanol can be oxidised to acetaldehyde, phenolics to quinones, and pigments and tannin polymerise or brown along the way. In winemaking it is both a tool and a risk, and the difference lies in whether it is controlled.

Definition and scope

The boundary of oxidation is reaction involving oxygen. It is not simply ageing, and it is not acetification driven by acetic acid bacteria. Ageing can happen in a low-oxygen environment, such as lees ageing or a sealed vessel; oxidation requires oxygen to keep entering. Acetification is a separate metabolic path in which acetic acid bacteria turn ethanol into acetic acid and need oxygen in order to multiply, so the two often occur together but are not the same thing.

A second boundary is "controlled" versus "accidental". Controlled oxidation is a process with a clear objective, such as Oloroso Sherry, tawny Port, Madeira and vin jaune; accidental oxidation is the result of inadequate protection, showing as early loss of fruit, browning, loose texture and, in serious cases, a vinegary smell.

Etymology and origin

Oxidation is formed from oxygen plus -ation. The name oxygen was proposed by the French chemist Lavoisier in the 1770s, from Greek oxys (sharp, acid) and genos (producing), because early chemistry regarded oxygen as the acid-forming element. Chinese uses a semantic translation. The wine trade also uses oxidative and maderisation to distinguish a deliberate style from a defect.

The vocabulary reflects a change in understanding: from an "element of burning and acid" to electron-transfer reactions, oxidation came to be seen as an exchange between phenolics, alcohols and oxygen rather than simply "going bad through contact with air".

Mechanism and parameters

Oxygen reaches wine through several routes: headspace in a vessel, slow transmission through oak staves, turbulence during racking and pumping, and air contact after opening. Warmer temperatures speed the reactions; at higher pH the active proportion of sulfur dioxide falls, protection weakens and oxidation accelerates; wines rich in phenolics, especially reds, buffer oxygen more but also change more when oxygen is excessive.

Acetaldehyde is the key intermediate. Ethanol oxidises to acetaldehyde, which at low levels may read as nutty, over-ripe apple or honey, and at high levels becomes a clear oxidative fault. Colour change is the most visible: white wines move from pale yellow-green to gold and amber, reds from purple-red to brick. An oak barrel admits oxygen at a rate often estimated in the tens of milligrams per litre per year, far above a stainless-steel tank, so barrel ageing is itself a slow oxidation.

Use in the industry

Controlled oxidation has many examples. Oloroso Sherry is fortified to about 17% alcohol after fermentation, which prevents flor growth and lets the wine age in constant contact with oxygen; vin jaune is aged in oak without topping up or racking, develops a yeast veil on the surface and must spend at least 6 years and 3 months in cask; tawny Port and Madeira also rely on long oxidative ageing for nutty, caramel and spicy notes. Micro-oxygenation introduces small, slow amounts of oxygen during red-wine making to help tannins polymerise and colour stabilise.

Protection is equally concrete: topping up to reduce headspace, inert gas, control of dissolved oxygen, appropriate sulfur dioxide and lower storage temperature. A wine left open should have its headspace reduced and be kept cold; decanting deliberately introduces oxygen, which can help a young, tightly built wine but may accelerate the decline of a fragile old one.

Terms easily confused

  • Oxidation versus acetification: oxidation is the reaction of oxygen with phenolics and alcohols; acetification is acetic acid bacteria turning ethanol into acetic acid. They often occur together, but the mechanisms and controls differ.
  • Oxidation versus ageing: ageing can include oxidation, but ageing is not oxidation; reductive ageing on lees or in a sealed vessel also develops flavour.
  • Oxidative versus oxidised: oxidative describes a deliberate oxidative style such as Oloroso, while oxidised describes a wine that has already developed a fault. The same chemistry carries opposite evaluations.
  • Maderisation versus oxidation: maderisation is a specific style process involving heat and oxygen, not a synonym for all oxidation.

Misconceptions and disputes

First, all oxidation is treated as a fault, ignoring that controlled oxidation is a mature style. Second, "old-wine smell" is equated with oxidation; the complexity of old wine can come from esters, terpenes and integration over time rather than from acetaldehyde. Third, adding sulfur dioxide is assumed to prevent oxidation, ignoring dose, pH and container management. Fourth, a wine that deteriorates after opening is assumed to be oxidised only, when microbes and acetification may also be involved.

One dispute concerns the dose and effect of micro-oxygenation. Studies do not agree on the best range, which depends on wine structure, temperature and phenolic composition. Another dispute concerns the boundary between "controlled oxidation" and "fault", which depends on stylistic expectation: the same level of acetaldehyde is a feature in Sherry and a defect in a fresh white. Some also treat longer decanting as always better, ignoring how fragile old wines can be.

Typical figures and examples

  • Oloroso Sherry is fortified to about 17% alcohol, which suppresses flor and allows oxidative ageing.
  • Vin jaune must spend at least 6 years and 3 months in oak, without topping up or racking, under a yeast veil that partly limits oxygen.
  • White wine colour: pale yellow-green, then gold, then amber; red wine colour: purple-red, then brick.
  • Acetaldehyde is an intermediate of ethanol oxidation, nutty or over-ripe apple at low levels and an oxidative fault at high levels.
  • Example: a 750 mL bottle left half full has about 375 mL of headspace, greatly increasing the area and time of oxygen contact; refrigeration slows but cannot reverse the change.
  • General chemistry: reaction rates roughly double for every 10°C rise, so summer heat damages an open bottle faster.
