Aroma

Aroma is the volatile smell perceived by the nose, layered by origin into varietal, fermentation and maturation notes; it is not the same as flavour, and aroma is not a synonym for bouquet.

Aroma is the volatile smell perceived by the nose. It is the first piece of information in tasting and a key clue to a drink's type, condition and complexity. Aroma is not the same as flavour: aroma happens mainly in the nasal cavity, while flavour combines taste in the mouth with smell rising from the back of the throat. The two should be recorded separately.

Figure: Aroma. Image: Jan Brueghel the Elder / Peter Paul Rubens, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Definition and boundaries

Aroma describes “what is smelled”, not “what is tasted”. Professional tasting distinguishes orthonasal smelling (raising the glass to the nose) from retronasal smelling (aroma rising from the mouth after swallowing). The same drink can give different impressions by the two routes, so a tasting note should say how the aroma was assessed.

The boundary also lies between stylistic aroma and defective smell. Fruit, flower, spice, oak and maturation notes belong to style; oxidation, reduction, cork taint and bacterial spoilage belong to defects. Treating a defect as style, or a style note as a defect, distorts the judgement. Intensity of aroma is not direct evidence of quality either.

Etymology and origin

“Aroma” comes from Greek, where it meant spice, aromatic herb and sweet smell, and passed through Latin into English as a pleasant volatile odour. “Bouquet” comes from French, originally a bunch of flowers, and in wine language came to mean the complex smell formed by fermentation and ageing. The two words have different origins and do not fully overlap in meaning.

Modern sensory science layers aroma by origin: varietal aroma from the raw material, fermentation aroma from microbial metabolism, and maturation aroma from oak, bottle age and long storage. This is a classification of “where the smell comes from”, not a strict timeline. It helps tasters separate the contributions of grape, yeast and container.

Mechanism and parameters

Aroma comes from hundreds of volatile compounds, and concentration, threshold and combination determine perception. Monoterpenes such as linalool, geraniol and nerol often bring rose, lychee and floral notes; methoxypyrazines often bring green pea, green pepper and earthiness; esters such as isoamyl acetate often smell of banana; diacetyl often smells of butter. These compounds do not act alone but interact with alcohol, acidity and temperature.

Temperature, glass shape, aeration and the state of the mouth all change aroma. Low temperature suppresses volatiles, while high temperature amplifies alcohol and sweetness; a narrow glass rim concentrates aroma; aeration can open some wines but also let fragile notes escape too quickly. Smell first without swirling, then swirl gently, and record in two or three passes to reduce first-impression bias. Alcohol burn is a sensation, not an aroma, but high strength can mask delicate notes.

Practical use in the drinks industry

In wine tasting, aroma is often grouped into fruit, floral, herbal, spice, oak and maturation notes; producers also use aroma to judge ripeness, fermentation state and possible contamination. In whisky, cereal, fruit, floral, peat, wood and spice are common descriptors; in beer, the BJCP style guidelines treat aroma as a primary evaluation point, used to judge malt, hops, yeast and fermentation by-products.

Sake aroma is often described as rice, fruit (apple, banana) and lactic notes. Aroma vocabulary cannot be transferred freely between categories: applying a wine's “minerality” to beer often lacks a corresponding basis, and applying beer's “hop aroma” to sake misrepresents it. When comparing across categories, first confirm what the term means within that category.

Related terms compared

The first pair is aroma and bouquet. In common trade usage, aroma leans towards the raw, varietal smell from the grape, while bouquet leans towards the complex smell formed by fermentation and ageing; some authors use the two as synonyms, so formal writing should state which definition it follows. The second pair is aroma and flavour. Aroma is smell, flavour is taste and smell combined; a wine that smells of honey may not taste sweet.

The third pair is varietal aroma and fermentation aroma. The first comes from the raw material, the second from yeast and microbes; a fruity note may come from the grape or from esters, and “it smells like fruit” alone does not classify it as varietal. The fourth pair is aroma and alcohol sensation. The heat of high strength is a stimulus, not a smell; writing it into an aroma note strips the record of discriminating power.

Common misuses and debates

First, equating aroma with flavour. Second, equating strong aroma with high quality. Third, assuming more complexity is always better; some drinks are valued for cleanliness and directness. Fourth, treating alcohol burn as aroma. Fifth, using only vague words such as “aromatic” or “fruity” without recording specific smells.

Debate centres on words such as “minerality”. They are often used for slate, wet stone and salt, but a single compound is hard to identify, and tasters understand them differently. Another debate is the role of aroma dictionaries: memorising words provides a shared language but cannot replace actual smelling practice. Applying dictionary entries directly to a drink easily creates preconceived judgements.

Typical figures and examples

The petrol note in aged Riesling has a specific chemical explanation: TDN, or 1,1,6-trimethyl-1,2-dihydronaphthalene, develops from precursors in the grape during ageing; in moderation it adds complexity, in excess it can overwhelm the fruit. The green pepper note of Cabernet Sauvignon and related varieties is often linked to methoxypyrazines; diacetyl from malolactic fermentation and lees contact can give Chardonnay a buttery character.

When tasting Riesling, petrol, honey, citrus and wet stone are often recorded side by side; in beer, BJCP describes malt sweetness, hop aroma and yeast character separately. Linking specific compounds to sensory words gives more information than saying only that “the aroma is complex”.
