Semi-dry

Semi-dry sits between dry and semi-sweet or sweet; the exact residual sugar varies by category and regulation, and sweetness cannot be judged by taste alone.

Semi-dry (often written medium dry in EU labelling) is the middle residual-sugar band: above dry, below semi-sweet and sweet. That “middle” has no single global number, because still wine, sparkling wine and national rules each use their own ranges. The same phrase can point to very different sweetness in Champagne and in an ordinary still wine.

Definition and boundaries

At its core, semi-dry means residual sugar lies between dry and semi-sweet. In the EU still-wine labelling system, dry has an upper limit of 4 g/L; semi-dry (medium dry) normally means above 4 g/L and no more than 12 g/L, with the upper limit rising to 18 g/L when total acidity is at least 10 g/L; above that comes medium sweet and sweet. The band is therefore a conditional range, not one fixed number.

The boundary also depends on category. In sparkling wine, “semi-dry” corresponds to demi-sec at roughly 32–50 g/L, far above the still-wine band; Champagne brut is below 12 g/L, even lower than the still-wine semi-dry ceiling. Applying the still-wine standard to Champagne produces the wrong answer. Always confirm the category and the applicable rules first.

Etymology and origin

“Semi-” comes from Latin, meaning half or partly; “dry” in drinks language means not sweet. Together they mean “partly dry, lightly sweet”. The Chinese term is a direct translation of the idea, while the official EU term is medium dry, sometimes rendered as medium-dry in English. The same concept has different wording in different languages, so the label term matters more than a literal translation.

Semi-dry is not a fixed natural category but a label shaped by regulation and market practice. Early wine had no modern residual-sugar testing, and sweet versus dry was often described by taste; once sugar could be measured precisely, countries wrote sweetness bands into law. Today's ranges are the result of long adjustment among measurement technology, consumer habits and trade rules.

Mechanism and parameters

Residual sugar is the first parameter, but perceived sweetness also depends on acidity, alcohol, temperature, carbon dioxide and aroma. High acidity makes the same amount of sugar taste drier; high alcohol increases the sense of sweetness and weight; low temperature and bubbles suppress sweetness; intense fruit aroma can make a wine “smell sweeter”. A wine at 8 g/L may seem dry in one setting and semi-dry in another.

For measurement, residual sugar is usually expressed as glucose in g/L; enzymatic methods, HPLC and refractometric estimates can give slightly different values. Label bands generally refer to the method specified by the regulation. Total acidity and pH must be recorded too, because the EU semi-dry ceiling rises to 18 g/L when total acidity reaches 10 g/L; looking at sugar without acid can misclassify the band.

Practical use in the drinks industry

In still wine, semi-dry is used for everyday drinking, food pairing and entry-level sweet styles; producers may stop fermentation before it finishes, or add a small amount of sweetener or unfermented grape juice to reach the target residual sugar. EU labelling imposes explicit conditions on “medium dry”, so a producer designing a back label must check residual sugar, total acidity and category together.

In sparkling wine, semi-dry corresponds to demi-sec, common in sweeter Champagne and sparkling wines; its residual-sugar range is completely different from still wine. Champagne sweetness also involves the dosage added after disgorgement: brut nature requires less than 3 g/L and no sugar after disgorgement, while brut is below 12 g/L. Mixing these terms with semi-dry leads to errors in pairing and buying.

Related terms compared

The first pair is semi-dry and dry. Dry usually has a ceiling of 4 g/L, and semi-dry begins above 4 g/L; the difference is a measurable residual-sugar line, not merely a matter of taste. The second pair is semi-dry and semi-sweet. EU still-wine semi-sweet usually starts at 12 g/L (18 g/L when acidity is high), and semi-dry ends at the same line; the two terms cannot be swapped by impression.

The third pair is semi-dry and Champagne demi-sec. The former is the middle band for still wine, the latter a high-sweetness band for sparkling wine; the same translated word may cover different gram figures. The fourth pair is semi-dry and “not sweet”. A wine may taste dry because of high acidity while its residual sugar still falls in the semi-dry band; when label and taste disagree, the measured value decides. The fifth pair is semi-dry and sweet. Sweet usually exceeds 45 g/L, far above semi-dry.

Common misuses and debates

First, judging semi-dry by taste alone; acidity, temperature and bubbles all deceive the tongue. Second, applying the still-wine band to Champagne. Third, treating semi-dry as semi-sweet. Fourth, ignoring the effect of total acidity on the upper limit. Fifth, assuming lower residual sugar means higher quality; sweetness is a style choice, not a quality grade.

Debate centres on the gap between label and perception. Consumers often read “it does not taste sweet” as “dry”, but the regulation looks at residual sugar; a high-acid, cold, sparkling wine may taste very dry while its residual sugar is above 4 g/L. Another debate is that semi-dry is used inconsistently across countries, and a Chinese back label on an imported wine may not match the original term exactly; buyers should rely on the original label and official category.

Typical figures and examples

For EU still wine: dry is no more than 4 g/L; semi-dry is above 4 g/L and no more than 12 g/L, with the ceiling at 18 g/L when total acidity is at least 10 g/L; semi-sweet is usually 12–45 g/L, and sweet is above 45 g/L. A wine with 8 g/L residual sugar and 6 g/L total acidity is semi-dry; the same 8 g/L with 11 g/L acidity is also semi-dry, because the ceiling remains 12 g/L.

The acidity allowance shows clearly at 15 g/L residual sugar: with total acidity at 11 g/L it can still be labelled semi-dry; with total acidity at only 6 g/L it becomes semi-sweet. For Champagne, brut is below 12 g/L, extra dry 12–17 g/L, sec 17–32 g/L, demi-sec 32–50 g/L and doux above 50 g/L. Placing these figures side by side shows the gap between the two meanings of “semi-dry”.
