GB/T 15109-2021 Terminology of baijiu industry

GB/T 15109-2021 is the current Chinese national standard for terminology in the baijiu industry. It defines basic terms used in production, testing, labelling, trade and industry communication. It is not a product-quality grading standard, and it does not replace product standards, analytical method standards or food-safety standards.

The standard has been revised more than once. It was first published as GB/T 15109-1994, revised in 2008, and this 2021 edition is the second revision. The version history matters because terminology changes with production methods, analytical techniques and market usage; citing an older definition without its version number can create confusion.

What a terminology standard does

The baijiu industry has long faced two related problems: one concept may have several names, and one name may refer to different objects in different regions or companies. Terms such as qu, fermented grain, fermentation pit, base liquor and blending carry meanings that are not always identical across texts. A terminology standard fixes core concepts to definitions that can be cited consistently, reducing ambiguity between production records, test reports, product labels and consumer communication.

A terminology standard usually does not set a target for a particular product. It answers “what does this word mean”. Seeing “GB/T 15109-2021” on a page should therefore not be read as “this bottle reached a certain quality grade”; it means that the terms used follow that standard's vocabulary. Quality grades, hygiene limits and analytical methods belong to the relevant product and method standards.

How it relates to the beverage classification standard

GB/T 15109-2021 is specific to the baijiu industry. GB/T 17204-2021, Terminology and classification of alcoholic beverages, covers the wider field and provides the framework for alcoholic beverages and their categories. The two standards can be used together: first establish the broad category under the beverage standard, then describe baijiu-specific concepts under the baijiu terminology standard. Readers should keep the scope of each standard in view rather than applying a broad category definition directly to a specific product.

Points to watch

First, the standard text is protected by copyright. This standard records only public metadata such as the standard code, title, dates and technical committee, and explains the standard's role in original words. It does not reproduce the standard. For exact definitions, consult the official full-text system or a formally published copy.

Second, write the full standard code. GB/T 15109 has editions from 1994, 2008 and 2021. Omitting the year merges different editions. In articles, labels or test reports, give both the code and the edition year.

Third, terminology consistency is not factual proof. Whether a product actually used a particular process cannot be established only from the vocabulary it uses; production records, label statements and verifiable sources are also needed. This site keeps standard information separate from descriptions of specific drinks, brands and regions so that “terminology compliance” is not misreported as “quality certification”.

In editorial practice, a terminology entry is best treated as an “explanation entry point”. A reader may meet an unfamiliar word on a bottle label, in a news story or in a video, then come to the term page to confirm what it means. A term page should therefore give the definition first, then the boundary, then examples; it should not stuff brand stories, regional history and tasting notes into one page. Once the definition is clear, specific drinks and regions can expand separately, with internal links connecting them.

For a content team, the standard also imposes a practical constraint: one article must not use conflicting definitions at the same time. “Solid-state fermentation”, for example, should not mean a process route in one sentence and a product category in the next; “base liquor” should not mean unblended liquor in one place and finished product in another. Editors should maintain a project glossary that fixes the definitions, edition and English rendering in use, and check it before writing. This is more efficient than explaining the same term anew in every article and less likely to produce contradictions.

When comparing the 1994, 2008 and 2021 editions, general readers do not need a clause-by-clause comparison, but they should know that differences may come from two sources: changes in industry practice and changes in drafting rules. A definition in older material may still “look reasonable” while no longer being the current wording. When citing an older edition, write “under GB/T 15109-1994” or “under GB/T 15109-2008” explicitly, not simply “the national standard”. Likewise, when translating an older term, state which edition is being followed.

This site's approach is: precise metadata, clear explanation, conservative judgement. Standard codes, titles, dates and technical committees follow the official pages; term meanings are explained in original words without reproducing the standard; questions about a specific product's compliance are limited to verifiable information, without substituting for the regulator. This gives readers useful background while avoiding the mistake of treating a terminology explanation as legal advice.

A reader who wants a quick reliability check can ask three questions: which edition is cited? Is the statement a terminology definition or a product requirement? Is there a clickable source? If none can be answered, the passage is better treated as a lead than as a conclusion.
