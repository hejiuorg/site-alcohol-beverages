Bordeaux AOC Decree

Bordeaux is a regional appellation, recognised in 1936, with a specification approved in September 2025. It sets grape varieties, yield ceilings, pruning and labelling for red, white and rosé wines, and sits above dozens of more specific village-level AOCs.

Burgundy AOC Decree

Burgundy has 84 appellations in three tiers — regional, village and grand cru — with parcels traced down to historical units called climats. In 2015 the climats were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site, giving a set of vineyard names a cultural identity.

On this page · 8Read StandardsReviewedEN

Champagne AOC: The Appellation Rules

Champagne was recognised as an appellation d'origine contrôlée on 29 June 1936, with its area delimited by the law of 22 July 1927 and supervised today by INAO. The current specification requires seven main varieties, in-bottle second fermentation and at least 15 months' ageing for non-vintage and 36 months for vintage Champagne.

China's geographical indication protection

China's geographical indication product protection is governed by the Measures for the Protection of Geographical Indication Products (CNIPA Order No. 80), in force from 2024-02-01; the product must originate in a specific region and its quality, reputation or other characteristics must be essentially attributable to that region's natural and human factors, with authenticity, territoriality, specificity and linkage.

Cognac AOC Decree

Cognac was among the first French wine regions to be delimited, and its current product specification was approved and published in October 2022. It fixes six growing crus, permitted varieties, double copper-still distillation and oak ageing, and writes VS, VSOP and XO into law.

EU Wine Regulation 1308/2013

The European Union's framework for the common organisation of the wine market. Adopted in December 2013 and applicable from January 2014, it harmonises planting authorisations, geographical indications, labelling and trade. It does not tell a winemaker how to ferment; it defines when a wine may use a name, and what the bottle must disclose.

GB 2757-2012 National food safety standard — Distilled spirits and compounded spirits

A mandatory national food safety standard published on 2012-08-06 and effective from 2013-02-01, replacing GB 2757-1981; it covers distilled spirits and compounded spirits and limits methanol to 0.6 g/L for grain-based products and 2.0 g/L for other raw materials, and cyanide as HCN to 8.0 mg/L, both calculated on a 100% alcohol basis. It also requires alcohol content and the warning about excessive drinking on labels. Lead is not set here; it falls under GB 2762.

The current Chinese national standard for Nongxiangxing (strong-aroma) baijiu, published on 2021-03-09 and effective from 2022-04-01; it fully replaces GB/T 10781.1-2006 and forms Part 1 of the "Quality requirements for baijiu" series.

GB/T 10781.2-2022 Product quality requirements for baijiu — Part 2: Light-aroma baijiu

Published on 2022-07-11 and effective from 2023-02-01, this quality requirement replaces GB/T 10781.2-2006; it removes the high-strength and low-strength product classification, introduces special, premium and first grades, sets alcohol at 21.0%vol to 69.0%vol, and applies different acid and ester indices within one year and after one year of production.

Published on 2024-05-28 and effective from 2025-06-01, this quality requirement fully replaces GB/T 26760-2011; it writes high-temperature qu production, two rounds of grain feeding and multi-round stacking and pit fermentation into the standard, converts acidity and ester indices to a 53.0%vol basis, and sets separate limits for the first year after production and for later storage.

GB/T 14867-2007 Feng-flavour baijiu

A 2007 national standard for Feng-flavour baijiu replacing GB/T 14867-1994; its compound aroma is led by ethyl acetate with ethyl hexanoate as support, earthen pit fermentation and jiu-hai storage are process markers, and Shaanxi Xifeng is the representative product. The standard was replaced in full by GB/T 10781.6-2025 with effect from 2026-08-01.

GB/T 23547-2009 Strong-sauce mixed-aroma baijiu

Published on 2009-04-14 and effective from 2009-12-01, this standard defines strong-sauce mixed-aroma baijiu by a harmonious combination of strong and sauce character, classifies it by alcohol content into high-strength and low-strength liquor with premium and first grades, and lists Hubei Baiyunbian and Anhui Kouzijiao among representative products. GB/T 4927-2008 Beer

The Chinese beer product standard, published on 2008-12-29 and effective from 2009-10-01, replacing GB 4927-2001; it defines beer as a low-alcohol carbonated fermented drink made mainly from malt and water with hops and yeast, classifies it by colour and special styles, and sets alcohol, original wort extract, total acid, carbon dioxide and diacetyl limits. Only clauses 3.1 and 8.1 were originally mandatory, and the whole standard became recommended GB/T in 2017.

German Beer Purity Law

On 23 April 1516 the Dukes of Bavaria issued an edict at Ingolstadt limiting beer to barley, hops and water. Its principle now sits in section 9 of the German Provisional Beer Law, making it one of the few early-modern ingredient rules still enforced.

German Wine Law

Germany's wine law was last overhauled in 2021, with a new origin hierarchy applying from the 2026 harvest. It keeps four quality tiers and six Prädikat levels while shifting emphasis from ripeness to place, organised around 13 Anbaugebiete.

Italian DOCG System

DOCG is the top tier of the Italian wine pyramid. Created with the DOC system in 1963 and first awarded in 1980, it requires at least 7 years as a DOC, bottles of no more than 6 litres, batch-by-batch chemical and sensory testing, and a state seal.

Japan's Liquor Tax Law

Japan's Liquor Tax Law (Law No. 6 of 1953) is the national statute that defines liquor, classifies it, requires manufacturing licences and imposes liquor tax; tax is levied by volume on each category and alcohol strength, and a new National Tax Agency rate table applies from 2026-10-01. It is not a labelling law and not the sake grading system.

Japanese Sake Labelling

Japan's National Tax Agency set the standards for sake quality labelling in 1989 by Notification No. 8. Eight specific names separate junmai, ginjo and honjozo styles, tied to polishing ratios, koji share and a cap on added brewing alcohol. The names are process claims, not a ladder of quality.

Mezcal NOM-070

Mexican Official Standard NOM-070-SCFI-2016 defines mezcal as a 100% agave distillate and splits it into Mezcal, Artesanal and Ancestral according to the equipment used for cooking, crushing, fermentation and distillation. Craft stops being an adjective and becomes a checklist.

Regulation (EU) 2019/787 on Spirit Drinks

Adopted on 17 April 2019 and applicable from 25 May 2021, this framework regulation replaced Regulation (EC) No 110/2008. It defines spirit drinks, sets a 15% vol floor, lists 44 categories in Annex I, and governs legal names, labelling, age statements and geographical indications.

Spanish DO System

Spain's Denominación de Origen system was set by the 2003 vine and wine law, with DO, DOCa, VP and IGP tiers. A region must first exist for 5 years as a geographical-indication wine before applying for DO; DOCa requires 10 years as a DO and bottling inside the zone.

Tequila NOM-006

Mexican Official Standard NOM-006-SCFI-2012 sets the conditions for using the name tequila: one agave species, a protected territory of 181 municipalities, and certification by the Tequila Regulatory Council. Botany, geography and labelling are welded onto a single word.

The Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 (SI 2009/2890)

UK statutory instrument in force from 23 November 2009. It defines Scotch Whisky as distilled in Scotland, matured in oak casks for at least three years, distilled below 94.8% vol and bottled at no less than 40% vol, and lays down five categories and five protected area names.

US AVA System

American Viticultural Areas are the US geographical label system for wine, created in 1978 with the first AVA approved in 1980. An AVA only draws a boundary; it does not regulate varieties, yields or winemaking. To name an AVA, at least 85% of the grapes must come from it.

On this page · 8Read StandardsReviewedEN

US Distilled Spirits Labeling: 27 CFR 5

Title 27, Part 5 of the US Code of Federal Regulations, enforced by the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau. It uses standards of identity to define what whisky, bourbon, gin and vodka must be made from, and then dictates what the label has to say. Read it and a bottle's name stops being a marketing choice.
