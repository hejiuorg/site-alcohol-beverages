Rice-aroma baijiu

A baijiu aroma type made mainly from rice with xiaoqu, through semi-solid fermentation, distillation, ageing and blending, with ethyl lactate and beta-phenylethanol as the main aroma compounds; the current standard is GB/T 10781.3-2025, with a unified 25.0%–68.0% ABV.

Rice-aroma is the lightest branch of Chinese baijiu: rice is the only material, xiaoqu is the koji, fermentation is semi-solid, distillation uses a pot still, and the aroma is led by ethyl lactate and beta-phenylethanol. It has no strong-aroma pit mud and no high-temperature multi-round sauce process, nor does it use the low-temperature daqu and earthen jars of light aroma. Understanding rice aroma means understanding a deliberate choice of "less is more".

A distilled spirit made from rice

Rice-aroma baijiu is made mainly from rice through fermentation and distillation. It differs from sake and from rice wine: sake is a fermented drink, not distilled; rice wine is usually a low-strength fermented drink; rice-aroma baijiu is distilled and can be strong. Its "rice aroma" is not the smell of raw rice but a clean, honeyed note formed by steamed rice, koji and fermentation esters.

That aroma relies on neither pit mud nor high-temperature koji. Rhizopus in xiaoqu saccharifies starch, yeast turns sugar into alcohol, and ethyl lactate and beta-phenylethanol formed during fermentation make up the main aroma. Clean aroma, a soft sweet entry and a crisp finish are the classic description; a clear pit or sauce note is a departure from the style.

Standard history and definition

The old standard was GB/T 10781.3-2006. The current standard is GB/T 10781.3-2025, Quality requirements for baijiu — Part 3: Mixiangxing baijiu, published on 25 April 2025, implemented on 1 May 2026 and wholly replacing GB/T 10781.3-2006.

The new standard defines rice-aroma baijiu as baijiu made mainly from rice, with xiaoqu as the saccharifying and fermenting agent, through semi-solid fermentation, distillation, ageing and blending, without direct or indirect addition of edible alcohol or colour, aroma and flavour substances not produced by its own fermentation. Compared with the old edition, it removes classification by strength, no longer separating high-strength and low-strength spirit, and sets a unified range of 25.0%–68.0% ABV.

That change matters. The old standard listed high-strength spirit at 41%–68% ABV and low-strength spirit at 18%–40% ABV; the new one drops the split, so strength no longer carries a high or low label. The emphasis moves to the truthfulness of materials, koji and process. For drinkers, the ingredient list and standard number matter more than the number on the bottle.

Guilin Sanhua and the southern rice belt

The representative region is Guangxi, with Guilin Sanhua as the core product. Guilin sits in northern Guangxi, with clear, soft Li River water and a humid climate suited to rice and xiaoqu; Sanhua uses rice, xiaoqu and Li River water, fermented semi-solid and distilled in a pot still. Outside Guangxi, Guangdong, Fujian, Hunan and other southern rice-growing areas also make rice-aroma or near-rice-aroma products.

A region is not a quality guarantee. Rice aroma looks simple but demands fresh rice, sound xiaoqu cultures, careful fermentation temperature and a controlled distillation fire. Aged rice brings stale notes, contaminated xiaoqu brings sourness, and too warm a fermentation loses the clean aroma. Treating "made in the south" as "authentic" ignores these details.

Choosing and steaming rice

Rice aroma uses rice, not sorghum. Rice starch is largely branched, sticky and easy to gelatinise after steaming, which suits xiaoqu saccharification; its lower protein and fat mean fewer off-note sources and a cleaner body. Rice does not keep well, however, and aged rice can develop fat oxidation notes, so distilleries prefer fresh, whole, clean grain.

Steaming must cook the rice through without turning it to mush. A raw core leaves starch ungelatinised, saccharification incomplete and yield low; overcooked rice sticks together, reduces air permeability and prevents even xiaoqu growth. The steamed rice is spread to cool before xiaoqu is mixed in; too hot a temperature kills Rhizopus and yeast, while too cool a start slows saccharification.

Xiaoqu: saccharification and fermentation in one

Xiaoqu is the soul of rice aroma. It is usually made with rice flour or rice bran as a carrier, inoculated with Rhizopus, yeast and other microbes, and formed into cakes or starter balls. Rhizopus produces amylase that breaks starch into glucose; yeast turns sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide. Saccharification and alcohol production happen in one system, without the complex bacterial community that daqu relies on.

The culture, inoculation rate, incubation temperature and storage time of xiaoqu directly affect yield and aroma. Weak Rhizopus means slow saccharification and high residual sugar; overly vigorous yeast means violent fermentation and coarse aroma; contamination brings sour, stale or bitter notes. Xiaoqu looks simple, but its microbiology is demanding, which is why rice aroma is easy to start and hard to master.

Semi-solid fermentation

Rice aroma uses semi-solid fermentation, see fermentation. Steamed rice and xiaoqu are first mixed and kept solid so that Rhizopus can grow and break down starch; water is then added and the mash turns liquid so that yeast can convert sugar into alcohol. This "solid first, liquid second" route balances saccharification efficiency with even fermentation.

Moisture, temperature and time need fine control. Too little water blocks both saccharification and fermentation; too much dilutes the mash and the aroma. Too warm a fermentation turns fast and acidic; too cool a one stalls and leaves residual sugar. Compared with solid-state fermentation, semi-solid transfers heat more evenly but is also easier to fail through oxygen lack or contamination.

Pot distillation, ageing and blending

Distillation uses a pot still, see distillation. The fermented mash is heated, alcohol and aroma compounds vaporise together and are condensed; heads and tails are cut away, heads being high in methanol and aldehydes and tails carrying off-notes, while the heart is kept. Pot distillation is less efficient than continuous distillation but retains more rice aroma and esters.

New spirit is aged in clay jars so sulphides escape, the body softens and aromas integrate. Rice aroma does not sell on long ageing; most products stress freshness and clarity, and too long a rest can flatten the rice note or add wood and oxidation. Blending combines batches and strengths, with a little old spirit for lift when needed, but no external flavouring.

Key parameters

  • Current standard: GB/T 10781.3-2025, implemented on 1 May 2026.
  • Material: rice as the main raw material.
  • Koji: xiaoqu.
  • Process: semi-solid fermentation, distillation, ageing, blending.
  • Strength: 25.0%–68.0% ABV, with no high/low split in the new standard.
  • Main aroma compounds: ethyl lactate, beta-phenylethanol.

These parameters come from the national standard and public reporting and describe category boundaries. Strength, age and origin cannot each stand for quality; a clean aroma, a soft sweet body and a crisp finish are the real tests.

Boundaries with sake, rice wine and te-aroma

The difference from sake lies in distillation and koji. Sake is a fermented drink made from rice, rice koji and water through parallel fermentation and is not distilled; rice-aroma baijiu is distilled, uses xiaoqu, semi-solid fermentation and a pot still. The difference from rice wine lies in strength and process. Rice wine is usually a low-strength fermented drink; rice-aroma baijiu is distilled and can be strong or mild. The difference from te-aroma lies in koji, fermentation and vessel. Te-aroma uses daqu cultured from flour, bran and distillers' grains, solid-state fermentation and red conglomerate stone pits; rice aroma uses xiaoqu, semi-solid fermentation and no stone pit.

The difference from light aroma is also worth noting. Light aroma uses low-temperature daqu, earthen jars and clean steaming and distillation, with ethyl acetate dominant and a drier aroma; rice aroma uses xiaoqu, semi-solid fermentation and pot distillation, with ethyl lactate and beta-phenylethanol dominant, a sweeter and more "rice" character. Both are clean, but the cleanliness comes from different sources.

Common misconceptions

First, that rice aroma is rice wine. Rice wine is fermented; rice aroma is distilled. Second, that rice aroma only comes in low strength. The new standard covers 25.0%–68.0% ABV, and strong versions exist. Third, that xiaoqu is simple and low quality. Its cultures and fermentation control are demanding. Fourth, that rice aroma improves with long ageing. Most products stress freshness, and long ageing can flatten the rice note. Fifth, that Guilin Sanhua equals all rice aroma. Brand and aroma category are not the same. Sixth, that no pit aroma means no flavour. Clean, soft and crisp is the style target.
