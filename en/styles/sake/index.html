Sake

A Japanese fermented rice drink made with rice, water, koji and yeast, classified by polishing ratio and brewing method.

Sake is a traditional Japanese alcoholic drink made from rice, water, koji mould and yeast. Like wine, it is fermented rather than distilled; alcohol is usually around 15% ABV, with some genshu or namazake higher.

Figure: Sake. Image: Intforce, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Definition and classification

Japanese liquor tax law defines sake as a fermented drink made from rice, rice koji and water, with an alcohol content below 22%. Sake differs from soju: soju is distilled, while sake has no distillation step. It also differs from Chinese rice wine and huangjiu, which share the rice base but use different starters, yeasts and process routes.

Sake categories are fixed by three dimensions: the polishing ratio (how much of the grain is milled away), whether brewer's alcohol is added, and whether the sake is made by the low-temperature ginjo method. The category words on a label are legal terms, not marketing adjectives, so reading them is more useful than memorising brands.

History

Sake brewing goes back to the arrival of rice farming and fermentation techniques in Japan. In the early period, temples and shrines controlled brewing, and by the Muromachi period commercial breweries had appeared. In the Edo period, three-stage preparation, pasteurisation and winter brewing took shape, along with the toji system that organised the workforce.

From the Meiji era, sake modernised: yeasts were isolated and selected, temperature control and testing arrived, and liquor tax law began to regulate categories and labelling. In the late 20th century, ginjo techniques spread, and cool, long fermentation brought floral and fruity aromas. In the 21st century, milling equipment and cold chains improved the consistency of junmai daiginjo, and sake shifted from domestic consumption towards exports.

Raw materials and milling

Sake is made from rice, water, rice koji and yeast. Sake rice differs from table rice: the grains are larger, the starchy core is distinct, and protein and fat are lower. Common varieties include Yamadanishiki, Gohyakumangoku and Miyamanishiki. The outer layers of the grain contain protein and fat that cause off-flavours, so the rice is milled.

The polishing ratio is the proportion of the grain remaining after milling; 50% means half the grain has been removed, and a lower number means fewer off-flavour sources but higher cost. After milling, the rice is washed, soaked and steamed to a state that is firm outside and soft inside, so the koji mould can grow. Water is treated as a style element in Japanese sake, and hardness affects fermentation speed and aroma direction.

Category system

  • Junmai: only rice, water, koji and yeast, with no added brewer's alcohol; usually a fuller flavour.
  • Honjozo: a small amount of brewer's alcohol is added to lift aroma and texture, with a legal polishing ceiling of 70%.
  • Ginjo: fermented longer at lower temperature, often with floral and fruit notes, with a polishing ceiling of 60%; daiginjo has a ceiling of 50%.
  • Junmai ginjo / junmai daiginjo: meet both the junmai and ginjo conditions, combining junmai's body with ginjo's aroma.

There are also categories defined by handling, such as namazake, namachozo, genshu and koshu. Categories are not ranked in absolute quality; they differ in ingredient constraints and style aims. Read the category word together with the polishing ratio rather than the word "daiginjo" alone.

Production

The process is: mill, wash, soak, steam, make koji, build the yeast starter, carry out three-stage preparation, ferment, press, clarify, pasteurise, store and bottle. Making koji means inoculating steamed rice with koji mould so that starch breaks down into fermentable sugar; the yeast starter creates an acidic environment for yeast; three-stage preparation adds steamed rice, koji and water in three steps so fermentation builds gradually.

Qu making and starter management set the fermentation's starting point. Traditional kimoto starters rely on lactic acid bacteria for natural acidification, taking longer and giving complex flavour; sokujo starters add lactic acid to shorten the cycle and improve consistency. The mash usually ferments for about 20 to 30 days at 5 to 15°C, adjusted to the category.

Flavour and tasting

Sake aromas include rice, koji, lactic notes and floral-fruit notes. Junmai leans grain and cream, ginjo leans apple, pear and banana-like esters, and aged sake shows nut and dried-fruit notes. The palate is described through sweet, dry, sour, bitter and astringent impressions; Japan often uses the sake meter value to indicate the sweet-dry tendency, but a number cannot replace actual tasting.

In tasting, check clarity and colour, then smell: is the aroma rice-and-koji led or fruit led? On the palate, judge the relationship between the water-like texture and the body; sake is usually softer than wine, with lower acidity. Temperature changes the aroma markedly: the same sake at 10°C and 45°C is almost two different experiences.

Drinking and pairing

Sake can be chilled, at room temperature or warmed. Ginjo styles usually suit 5–10°C to preserve aroma; junmai and fuller styles can be warmed to 40–55°C, and overheating brings out alcohol and bitterness. Traditional masu and ochoko cups have their uses, and glass shape affects aroma concentration, but the rules need not be absolute.

At the table, sake suits sushi, sashimi, grilled dishes, tofu and light seafood; the fruitiness of ginjo works with shellfish and white fish, while the rice notes of junmai suit simmered and soy-flavoured dishes. Strong spices and heavy chilli overwhelm sake's delicate aroma. Refrigerate after opening and finish within a few weeks; namazake especially needs cold storage.

Common misconceptions

First, confusing sake with shochu. Sake is fermented; shochu is distilled. Second, that a lower polishing ratio is always better. Low ratios bring aromatic potential and cost, and whether the style suits you matters more. Third, that warming is a low-end serve. Warming is a traditional way to drink sake, and the temperature depends on the product. Fourth, that daiginjo must be junmai. Daiginjo may include a little brewer's alcohol; junmai daiginjo is the additive-free counterpart. Fifth, ignoring storage. Sake fears light and heat, and namazake and unpasteurised products need a cold chain.
