Crème de Cassis

A deep purple blackcurrant liqueur made by macerating fruit in neutral spirit and sweetening it; Dijon is its historic centre, and its high sugar and high acid make it the soul of Kir and Kir Royale.

Crème de cassis is a deep purple liqueur made by steeping blackcurrants in neutral spirit and then sweetening the infusion. In French liqueur naming, crème does not mean cream; it marks a sweeter, thicker grade. The drink therefore contains no dairy, yet it often feels heavier than an ordinary fruit liqueur. Its most famous use is with white wine or sparkling wine in Kir and Kir Royale, but it also works over ice or spooned onto dessert.

A Deep Purple Definition

Structurally, crème de cassis is spirit plus fruit maceration plus sugar, with no fermentation. It needs enough sugar to carry the sharp acidity of blackcurrant and enough alcohol to fix the aroma. The colour comes from anthocyanins in skin and flesh, ranging from deep purple-red to almost black; a swirl leaves a visible purple tear on the glass.

It differs from blackcurrant syrup because it contains alcohol, from blackcurrant wine because it is not fermented, and from other members of the crème de fruits family only in its raw material. The word cassis is simply French for blackcurrant, and the product is also sold in Britain, the United States and Japan under the same French name.

Sugar and Geographical Indications

EU law gives blackcurrant liqueur its own category: at least 15% ABV and at least 400 grams of sugar per litre, expressed as invert sugar. That is far above the threshold for an ordinary liqueur and explains both the syrupy texture and the long shelf life. High sugar does not automatically mean cloying, because blackcurrant is itself very acidic; sugar and acid pull against each other and keep the finish from collapsing.

Geography is the other boundary. Dijon has protected "Cassis de Dijon" since 1925, requiring key production steps to take place in the city; Burgundy has registered "Crème de cassis de Bourgogne" as a French geographical indication since 2015. The origin wording on a label often says more about style than the brand name does.

Dijon: Accident and Necessity

Dijon is famous for mustard, yet it became the capital of blackcurrant liqueur in the nineteenth century. In 1841, Auguste-Denis Lagoute produced the first commercial version in the city, combining local fruit with neutral alcohol and sugar. The Lagoute and Lejay families later took the drink to Parisian cafés, and Dijon developed a cluster of liqueur houses.

In the twentieth century, Félix Kir, mayor of Dijon, served blackcurrant liqueur mixed with Burgundy white wine, and the drink became known as Kir. It solved the problem of thin, low-acid white wine while turning a digestif into an aperitif. Replacing still wine with sparkling wine gives Kir Royale, a brighter drink in which bubbles lift the fruit.

From Orchard to Maceration Tank

The key raw material is blackcurrant, especially small, thick-skinned, high-acid, aromatic varieties such as Noir de Bourgogne. Fruit is harvested in high summer and crushed or left whole before going into a tank, with minimal delay to limit oxidation. Producers mix fruit with neutral alcohol for several weeks so that colour, acidity and berry aroma pass into the liquid, then press, filter and adjust sweetness and strength with sugar syrup.

Temperature and time shape the style: a long, cool maceration preserves fresh fruit, while a warmer, shorter one extracts more colour and tannin. Some producers keep a little suspended solids for texture; others filter completely clear. Either way, the aim is to keep blackcurrant acidity, sweetness and skin tannin on one line rather than letting sugar flatten the fruit.

Acid, Sugar and Tannin

In the glass it is deep purple-red with a violet rim. The nose begins with blackcurrant jam, mulberry and violet, then moves to pepper, undergrowth and a little yeast. The entry is sweet, but acidity follows immediately and cuts through it; the middle shows a slight grip from the skins, and the finish returns to berries and flowers. Strength usually sits between 15% and 20% ABV; too much alcohol buries the fruit, too little makes it syrupy.

Temperature matters. Chilled, the acidity is crisper and the drink works as an aperitif; at room temperature the aroma expands but sweetness feels heavier, which suits chocolate or blue cheese better. Three things are worth checking: whether the colour looks natural, whether acidity gives support, and whether the finish is clean. A product that is only sweet will tire the palate within a glass or two.

Dijon, Burgundy and the Brand Landscape

Lejay-Lagoute represents Dijon, tracing its recipe to 1841 and defending the city's origin; Gabriel Boudier and L'Héritier-Guyot are also rooted there, the former known for liqueurs and cocktail ingredients, the latter for fruit and dessert styles. Cartron and other Burgundy producers use the "Crème de cassis de Bourgogne" indication to stress local orchards and a fuller regional story.

Large merchants and supermarket own-brands offer cheaper versions, often blending fruit from different areas and standardising the sugar-acid balance. They are not necessarily bad, but their fruit tends to taste less vivid and their acidity less sharp when served neat or in Kir. When buying, look at origin, fruit variety and sugar level rather than the purple label alone.

Kir and Kir Royale: Two Ways to Serve

The classic Kir ratio is one part blackcurrant liqueur to four or five parts dry white wine, traditionally Burgundy Aligoté; the wine turns from pale gold to ruby, and the acidity of the cassis brightens its roundness. Kir Royale replaces still wine with sparkling wine, softening the sweetness and making the drink festive. Neither works well with a very sweet base wine.

Beyond mixing, crème de cassis can be served over ice, lengthened with soda, poured over vanilla ice cream or used as a sauce for chocolate desserts. With grape wine, acidity is the key: high-acid whites and dry sparkling wines sit more easily than sweet styles. In cocktails, remember to reduce the residual sugar elsewhere, or the total sweetness climbs quickly.

Why It Is Not a Fruit Wine

Crème de cassis is often mistranslated as "blackcurrant wine", but the processes differ. Fruit wine is made by fermenting juice or pulp, so its alcohol comes from converted sugar; in crème de cassis the alcohol comes from added spirit, while the fruit supplies aroma, colour and acidity, and sugar is added at the end. The former is usually lower in alcohol and more fermentation-driven; the latter is sweeter, more stable and easier to keep.

It is not blackcurrant juice or syrup either. Juice spoils after opening, whereas high sugar and high alcohol make the liqueur far more durable. That is why a recipe using it must adjust acid and sugar together rather than substituting it one-for-one for juice.

Buying and Storage

First check the category word: "crème de cassis" carries the higher sugar level, while "liqueur de cassis" is usually lighter. Then look at strength and origin; 15% to 20% is the common range, and Dijon or Burgundy origins often indicate a clearer source of fruit. Once opened, keep the bottle cool and out of direct light. High sugar and alcohol make it more robust than most fruit liqueurs, but a brown colour or a flat, oxidised smell means it is no longer at its best.
