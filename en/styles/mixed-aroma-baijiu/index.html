Mixed aroma baijiu

A baijiu aroma style that combines strong aroma and sauce aroma characters, represented by Baiyunbian and Kouzijiao; GB/T 23547-2009 applies, and aroma style is not a quality grade.

Mixed aroma baijiu is a baijiu aroma style that combines strong aroma and sauce aroma characters, often called strong-sauce mixed aroma. It is not a simple blend of two finished spirits. Instead it accommodates two process routes in qu making, fermentation and blending, producing a compound aroma. The current standard is GB/T 23547-2009, Strong aroma and sauce aroma mixed type baijiu.

Figure: Mixed aroma baijiu. Image: Xiaomage de, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Definition and standard

The standard describes mixed aroma baijiu as a baijiu with a compound strong and sauce aroma, and sets requirements for raw materials, process, sensory and physicochemical indices. Mixed aroma is a classification of aroma style, not a quality grade; products within one style still lean towards strong aroma, towards sauce aroma, or sit in between.

Mixed does not mean compromise. The difficulty of the style is letting two aroma families coexist without masking each other: the pit aroma and soft sweetness of strong aroma, and the roasted, full-bodied notes of sauce aroma, must find a proportion in the body. When the proportion tips, the product reads as muddled rather than more complex.

Regions and examples

Representative products include Baiyunbian from Songzi in Hubei and Kouzijiao from Huaibei in Anhui. Enterprises follow different routes: some build on a sauce aroma process and add strong aroma elements, some build on a strong aroma process and introduce high-temperature stacking and high-temperature qu, and others combine different base spirits to reach a mixed aroma.

A place name is not a quality guarantee. Raw materials, qu types, fermentation vessels and blending ratios differ widely between producers, and finished products can range from strong-leaning to sauce-leaning. Treating mixed aroma as one fixed flavour ignores the product tiers inside a company.

Raw materials and qu

Mixed aroma uses sorghum as its main material, and qu making often uses high-temperature daqu and medium-temperature daqu together, or different qu types across rounds. High-temperature qu brings sauce-like roasted and qu notes, medium-temperature qu keeps the ester and grain notes of strong aroma, and the ratio between them directly steers the style.

See the general principles of qu making. More qu types is not automatically better: the microbial communities and enzyme systems of different qu must match, or fermentation can drift into acid imbalance or clashing aromas. Matching raw material and qu is the first threshold of the style.

Process

Mixed aroma follows two common approaches. The first is process integration: high-temperature stacking, high-temperature fermentation and mud-pit continuous-grain mixing are used inside one production system, so strong and sauce elements form in the same fermenting grain. The second is base spirit combination: strong aroma and sauce aroma base spirits are made separately and then blended in proportion.

Either route involves fermentation temperature, vessel and round management. The mud pit supplies pit aroma and the environment for caproic-acid bacteria, see fermentation pit; high-temperature stacking and high-temperature qu supply sauce-like aroma precursors. Cuts during distillation and graded storage decide whether a clear compound aroma can form.

Flavour profile

Mixed aroma usually shows pit aroma, grain aroma, roasted notes and a light sauce note at the same time, entering soft-sweet or full-bodied, with a medium-full body and a relatively long finish. Strong-leaning versions stress pit aroma and soft sweetness, sauce-leaning versions stress roasted notes and body, and balanced versions aim at a seamless join between the two.

Compound aroma is not stacked aroma. A good mixed aroma should have clear layers and a clean finish, with the aroma families not fighting each other. The more complex the aroma, the more it needs a clean base spirit and stable fermentation; otherwise complexity becomes muddle, which is the most common failure of this style.

Tasting and serving

Taste from a small cup, first still to judge the ratio of pit aroma to roasted notes, then with a gentle swirl to check for clashes or gaps. On the palate, watch the balance of sweet, sour and bitter and the thickness of body, then whether the finish is coherent. Too warm amplifies alcohol and roasted notes, too cold suppresses the layers.

Mixed aroma suits heavier Chinese dishes such as braised foods, grills and red-braised cooking. High-strength products should be sipped slowly from small cups, and low-strength products should be kept dark and chilled after opening and finished soon. Judge strength by the label, not by the aroma style.

Common misconceptions

First, treating mixed aroma as strong aroma spirit simply blended with sauce aroma spirit. Process integration and base spirit combination are two different routes. Second, treating mixed aroma as a superior style. Aroma style is classification, not rank. Third, believing more complex aroma is always better. Layers and a clean finish matter more than stacking. Fourth, treating a strong-leaning or sauce-leaning profile as a defect. These are stylistic directions, not quality problems. Fifth, ignoring product tiers within a company. Mixed aroma products under one brand can differ greatly.
