Gin

A distilled spirit led by juniper, divided by production method and additives into London dry, distilled and compound gin.

Gin is a distilled spirit whose core flavour comes from juniper. Its technical skeleton is "neutral alcohol plus a botanical blend plus distillation"; its flavour skeleton is "juniper-led with the other botanicals modifying it". Understanding these two layers matters more than memorising a brand.

Figure: Gin. Image: Decatur Wine & Spirits, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Definition and regulation

EU regulation requires gin to show a clear juniper flavour, sets a minimum bottling strength of 37.5% ABV, and allows other botanicals in distillation. Gin is divided by production method into distilled gin, London gin (usually corresponding to London dry gin) and compound gin. The rules for "London gin" are stricter: the flavour must come from redistillation, the main flavour may not be created by adding essences afterwards, sugar may be used only to round the finish and must not exceed 0.1 g/l, and the product may not be coloured.

"London Dry" is not a proof of origin. It describes a production and flavour constraint, not a requirement that the gin be made in London. This is often misunderstood and is the most disputed point on gin labels. Buyers should read the category word and the strength rather than the place name alone.

History

Gin's predecessor is Dutch genever, a 16th-century grain distillate flavoured with juniper and originally tied to medicinal and spiced traditions. In the 17th century it reached England with trade and war and evolved into a drier spirit that emphasised botanical aroma. In the 18th century London saw large-scale cheap gin consumption, social problems and legislation, and this period shaped the cultural image of "gin and the city".

In the 19th century, continuous distillation and more stable mashing improved quality, and London dry gin gradually became an export standard. In the later 20th century gin consumption fluctuated, but the martini and the gin and tonic kept it relevant; the craft-gin wave of the 21st century brought more botanical blends and regional styles, widening gin from "classic dry" to a broader aromatic spectrum.

Raw materials and botanicals

The base is usually neutral grain alcohol, and the botanical recipe is centred on juniper with common partners such as coriander seed, angelica, orris root, citrus peel, liquorice, cardamom, cassia and almond. Juniper gives pine and resin, coriander gives citrus and spice, angelica and orris give earth and floral notes, and liquorice adds sweetness and a lingering finish. Proportions decide the front, middle and back of the aroma.

Recipes are usually not fully disclosed. The "number of botanicals" on a label is marketing information and cannot replace a judgment of balance. Juniper must be clearly recognisable, or the product is closer to flavoured vodka than to gin. Compared with vodka, gin's goal is not neutrality but a recognisable botanical identity.

Main categories

  • Distilled gin: neutral alcohol is redistilled with juniper and other botanicals; a small amount of flavouring may be added after distillation, but the main flavour must come from distillation.
  • London gin / London dry gin: the flavour comes entirely from redistillation, no essences may be added afterwards, sugar and colouring are restricted, and the style is usually drier and clearer.
  • Compound gin: botanicals are macerated or blended into alcohol without redistillation; the category is legal, but aromatic integration and complexity usually differ.

The three are not ranked in absolute quality; the difference is in production constraints and style. London dry suits cocktails that need structure, while compound gin suits lighter, more direct drinking.

Production

The public process is: prepare neutral alcohol, add the botanicals to the distillation system, heat and distil, make the cut between heads and tails, keep the balanced heart, adjust strength with water and bottle. Botanicals may be macerated before distillation or held in a basket so that steam passes through them, and the two methods extract aroma with different efficiency.

Still shape, heating rate and cut points all affect style. Higher reflux gives a lighter, cleaner body; a lower cut keeps more oils and aroma but risks roughness. Batch variation comes from agricultural materials and distillation and is normal. The producer's core task is to keep juniper and the supporting botanicals in balance in the same glass.

Flavour and tasting

Typical gin aromas are juniper, pine, citrus peel, coriander and floral notes; the entry is usually dry, the alcohol is clear, and the finish carries bitterness and spice. Styles differ widely: some lean citrus and floral, some pine and pepper, some sweeter and rounder. When tasting, find juniper first, then see whether the supporting botanicals serve the main theme.

Method: a small amount neat in a small glass; a few drops of water to see whether the aroma opens; then ice and tonic to compare how bubbles, sweetness and bitterness affect the botanical detail. Do not evaluate gin in large mouthfuls, because high strength masks aromatic layers.

Drinking and pairing

Gin and tonic is the most common serve, with a gin-to-tonic ratio usually between 1:2 and 1:3, plenty of ice and lime or lemon peel. A martini puts the emphasis on the gin itself, with very little dry vermouth; the Negroni combines equal parts gin, bitter and sweet vermouth, with juniper supporting the bitter-sweet structure. Gin also works in a highball or with grapefruit.

Before a meal it pairs with olives, nuts, savoury snacks and seafood; the bitterness and bubbles of a gin and tonic cut through fat. With very sweet desserts the sugar overwhelms juniper and citrus. As a cocktail base its strength strongly affects the balance of the finished drink, so sugar and acid in the recipe need adjusting.

Common misconceptions

First, that London dry gin must be made in London. It is a production category, not a geographical indication. Second, that gin is "vodka with flavourings". Gin is juniper-led and its flavour structure differs from neutral vodka. Third, that more botanicals are better. Botanical count is not complexity, and balance and proportion matter more. Fourth, that all gin suits a martini. Styles vary widely, and citrus-forward and pine-forward gins behave differently in a martini. Fifth, ignoring strength and version. Markets and versions can differ in specification, and the label is authoritative.
