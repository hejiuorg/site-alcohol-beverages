Stout

A top-fermented dark beer given colour and coffee-cocoa flavour by roasted barley, named after stronger porter, represented by Dublin dry stout and split into sweet, oatmeal and imperial stout.

Stout is a dark top-fermented beer, characterised by the coffee, cocoa and roast aromas that roasted barley brings. The name comes from 18th century “stout porter”, a stronger porter; in the 19th century Dublin developed the dry stout form, and in the 20th century sweet stout, oatmeal stout and imperial stout branched off. Stout is not a synonym for “the strongest beer”, and the family spans a wide range of strengths.

Figure: Stout. Image: 55belgianbeerfan, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Definition and standards

Within the beer style system, stout is usually divided into dry stout, sweet stout, oatmeal stout and imperial stout. Dry stout commonly has around 4.0–5.0% alcohol and about 30–45 IBU; imperial stout often exceeds 8% alcohol, with richer, sweeter flavour and a fuller body. Classification rests on recipe intent and strength, not on how dark the beer is.

The colour range runs from deep brown to near black, but colour alone does not define the style. A strong ale loaded with dark malt need not be called stout, while a lighter-coloured dry stout can still belong to the family. When reading a label, alcohol content, recipe description and attenuation are more useful than a colour chart.

Origin and history

In the early 18th century London porter won the market with dark malt and extended ageing, and a stronger “stout porter”, the ancestor of stout, followed. In the late 18th century Dublin brewing began to produce porter and stout on a larger scale. In 1759 Arthur Guinness leased the St. James's Gate brewery, and the company gradually turned porter and stout into products for overseas markets. Today's Guinness Draught is the most familiar example of dry stout, brewed in Ireland.

In the 19th century Russian imperial stout travelled as a strong, heavily hopped export beer to the Baltic and Russian markets. In the 20th century British brewers developed sweet stout with lactose and oatmeal stout with oats. These branches usually grew out of the availability of ingredients, transport conditions and changing consumer taste.

Ingredients and process

Barley is the backbone of stout. Base pale malt supplies enzymes and fermentable sugar, unmalted roasted barley brings colour and coffee-cocoa flavour, and black malt may also be used. The degree of roasting and the share of roasted grain determine colour, bitterness and roast quality. A high proportion of roasted grain makes mashing and lautering harder, so the recipe needs balance.

Fermentation is top-fermenting, usually at 18–22°C. Dry stout aims for high attenuation, keeping the body light to medium and the finish dry; sweet stout adds lactose, which brewer's yeast cannot ferment, so residual sweetness and a smooth texture remain. Nitrogen dispensing changes foam texture through finer bubbles, but the flavour still comes mainly from malt and fermentation.

Modern craft beer has extended stout into barrel-aged versions and recipes with coffee, chocolate, vanilla or lactose. These additions are recipe innovations and do not change the basic structure of stout: dark roasted grain, top fermentation and a relatively complete malt backbone.

Flavour profile

The beer is deep brown to black, often with ruby edges in backlight. The aroma is dominated by coffee, dark chocolate and roasted bread, possibly with nuts, dried fruit or light caramel; hops usually provide only background bitterness. Dry stout is brisk on the palate, with clear roast bitterness and a dry finish; imperial stout is full-bodied, with sweetness, alcohol warmth and bitterness together; sweet stout is smoother and sweeter.

The roast in a good stout should be clean, without burnt, acrid smoke or cardboard notes. The latter two usually point to problems with ingredient handling or oxidation, not to style character. As the beer warms, alcohol and sweetness both become more obvious, which is why tasting benefits from temperature control.

Tasting and serving

Dry stout is best at around 6–10°C, while imperial stout can be served a little warmer at 12–16°C. Too cold suppresses coffee and cocoa aroma; too warm makes alcohol prominent. Glass shape affects foam and aroma, and a tulip or a narrowing glass both work; nitrogen versions have finer foam, and the pour should be allowed to form a head naturally.

With food, dry stout suits oysters, grilled meat and hard cheese, while imperial stout suits rich stews and dark chocolate desserts. Store away from light in a cool place, and check the production date on bottled products. Low-alcohol dry stout is best drunk fresh, while strong imperial stout can be cellared moderately under suitable conditions. Sweet stout with lactose is unsuitable for people with lactose intolerance.

Common misconceptions

First, assuming stout is always the strongest and heaviest. Dry stout often has alcohol close to that of ordinary lager. Second, treating stout and porter as opposites. They share a dark malt base and differ in strength, sweetness and roast level. Third, assuming a blacker colour means harsher roast. Colour comes from roasting; the quality of bitterness depends on process. Fourth, believing stout is highly nutritious or can supplement iron. Such folk claims lack reliable evidence. Fifth, assuming every stout benefits from long ageing. Only strong, well-structured versions have cellaring value.
