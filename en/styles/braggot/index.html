Braggot

A hybrid fermented from honey and malt, between mead and beer; hops may or may not be used, with styles from pale and light to dark and strong.

A mixed fermented drink of honey and malt, sitting between mead and beer; hops are optional and the range runs from pale and light to dark and strong.

Figure: Braggot: Swedish Mead (Wikimedia Commons). Image: Grapetonix, CC BY 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Definition and boundaries

Braggot is a drink in which honey and malt are fermented together, not a beer to which honey is added after fermentation. Honey can supply roughly 10–70% of the fermentable sugar, hops may be present or absent, and the finished drink ranges from pale and light to dark and strong. Alcohol commonly falls between 5% and 12% ABV, but the label rarely gives the honey-to-malt ratio that would explain the balance.

There is no single legal definition in the EU or the United States. Brewers and meaderies use the name according to their own formulas, so one “braggot” can be a lightly honeyed beer and another a honey-dominant mead with a malt backbone. The useful boundary is the fermentation: both honey and malt must contribute sugar, and the result must show both grain and honey character.

History and naming

Medieval English records already use the word braggot, and honey-and-malt fermentation was a common household practice in northern Europe and the British Isles. Early versions had no hops; herbs and spices often supplied bitterness and aroma. The name therefore predates modern beer categories and does not imply a fixed recipe.

Modern braggot was revived by craft meaderies and breweries. Some use malt as the base and honey at 10–20%; others invert the ratio and treat malt as the structural backbone. The BJCP places braggot within its mead categories, allowing dry through sweet and pale through dark versions, while commercial labels may use “honey beer” for a product that is not truly mixed-fermented.

The distinction matters because “honey beer” is often a beer with honey flavouring or a small honey addition. Braggot requires a genuine joint fermentation, and the honey should be visible in the aroma and texture rather than only on the ingredient list.

Materials and process

Malt is mashed first to make wort. Honey is dissolved in warm water, not boiling liquid, so its volatile floral compounds survive; the two liquids may be boiled together with hops or handled separately and blended before fermentation. Honey is low in yeast-available nitrogen and minerals, so a small nutrient addition is common to avoid a stalled ferment or sulphury off-flavours.

Yeast choice shapes the result. A neutral ale yeast keeps honey and malt aromas clear, lager yeast gives a cleaner profile, and wine yeast raises alcohol and acidity. Fermentation usually runs at 18–22°C, followed by a short cold settle, filtration or direct bottling. If residual sugar is high, bottling must wait until fermentation is demonstrably finished, or the bottle can continue fermenting and explode.

Honey variety changes the drink as much as the malt. Buckwheat honey is dark, mineral and caramel-like; acacia is pale and floral; citrus honey brings peel and white-flower notes. Blending two honeys is common because it balances aroma with a more stable fermentation. A producer that states honey percentage, malt type and hopping gives the reader far more information than a poetic label.

Style and palate

Pale braggot shows honey, white flowers, citrus and malt sweetness; dark versions bring caramel, bread, dried fruit and spice. Hopped braggot adds herbal and resinous notes, while spiced versions may have no hop bitterness at all. Sweetness ranges from bone dry to dessert sweet, body from medium to full, and carbonation from still mead-like to brisk and beer-like.

Compared with mead, braggot has a clearer grain skeleton and often more body. Compared with beer, honey lifts the floral aroma and adds a different kind of sweetness. The honey-to-malt ratio is the first thing to establish; hopping and sweetness come next. Judging from one bottle easily mistakes a formula difference for a style difference.

Dry braggot may finish below 1% residual sugar and rely on malt and honey proteins for body. Sweet versions can exceed 10% residual sugar and need careful bottling. Hopped examples often sit around 20–40 IBU, while spiced examples may use no hops. The same word therefore covers a dry, bitter drink and a rich, still one.

Representative regions and brands

The main producers are craft meaderies and breweries in the United States and the United Kingdom. Common styles include hopped braggot, festival versions with spice and dried fruit, and winter releases built on dark malt. Because braggot has no protected geographical name, reputation comes from the formula and the producer rather than from a region.

When buying, look for the honey source, malt type, alcohol and sweetness. A label that says only “honey beer” may describe a beer with honey added, not a mixed fermentation. American meaderies often name the honey variety; British producers more often emphasise malt and hops. Neither convention guarantees a particular ratio.

A useful comparison set is a pale hopped braggot, a dark malty braggot and a honey-dominant version. Keep alcohol and sweetness as close as possible so that the honey-to-malt balance, not the packaging, explains the difference.

How to drink and pair

Serve at 12–16°C in a tulip or stemmed glass, pouring a small amount so the aroma opens. Sweet braggot suits blue cheese, dried fruit and roast meat; dry braggot works with roast chicken, root vegetables and mild spice. Hopped versions can handle fried food, but do not chill them below 6°C or the honey aroma will close.

After opening, refrigerate and drink within two or three days. A braggot with residual sugar carries a real risk of continued fermentation, so store it upright and away from light and check for pressure before opening. High-honey versions are better served in small glasses than in beer-sized pours.

Carbonation can be very low, closer to still mead than to beer. Check for sediment before pouring and leave the last ounce in the bottle. A honey-dominant braggot rewards slow sipping; a beer-like version can be treated more like a strong ale.

Buying, storage and misreadings

Read the honey percentage and sweetness first, then the malt and hopping. “Honey beer” is not a synonym for braggot. A high price may reflect expensive honey or a small batch, not a more typical style. Store bottles upright, cool and dark, and treat bottle-conditioned versions as live products.

Three misreadings recur: treating braggot as simply “sweet beer”; assuming honey flavour means artificial flavouring; and treating a medieval recipe as a fixed formula. Braggot can be dry, bitter and hopped, and the defining point is the proportion of honey to malt in a joint fermentation.

Further reading and see also
