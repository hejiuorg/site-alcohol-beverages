Sherry

A fortified wine from the Jerez region of Spain, based mainly on Palomino and shaped either by biological ageing under flor (Fino, Manzanilla) or oxidative ageing (Oloroso, Amontillado, PX), then blended through the solera system.

Sherry is a fortified wine from the Jerez region of southern Spain. Its distinguishing feature is not simply that alcohol is added, but what happens afterwards. One path runs under a film of yeast called flor, producing the pale, bone-dry, bread-and-almond style of Fino. The other runs in contact with oxygen, producing the darker, nutty, caramel-scented style of Oloroso. Layered on top of both is the solera system, a fractional blending scheme that gives Sherry a very different sense of time from ordinary wine.

Definition and regulation

Sherry is a protected denomination of origin. The appellation covers the Jerez-Xérès-Sherry DO and the Manzanilla-Sanlúcar de Barrameda DO in Andalusia. Only wine made, fortified and matured within the defined area may carry the names Sherry, Jerez or Xérès.

The base wine is dry white wine, and fortification takes place after fermentation. This separates Sherry from Port: Port is fortified mid-fermentation to keep sugar, while Sherry is fortified after fermentation and is therefore naturally dry. Strength depends on style. Fino and Manzanilla are usually raised to 15%–15.5% ABV; Oloroso to 17%–18% or more. Sweet styles receive sweet wine or concentrated grape juice.

EU wine law provides the framework for fortified wines, geographical indications and labelling, while the DO specification sets the detailed rules on varieties, minimum strength, maturation and sweetness. Words such as Fino, Oloroso and PX are legal categories, not loose tasting notes.

History

Viticulture around Jerez goes back to the Phoenicians and Romans. Under Moorish rule the region developed irrigation and vineyards; after the Christian conquest in the thirteenth century, wine trade with England grew. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, Sherry reached the English table as "sack" and appeared in Shakespeare's plays.

British merchant houses established bodegas and ageing cellars in Jerez in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and exports expanded. Phylloxera devastated the region at the end of the nineteenth century, and replanting on grafted rootstock eventually restored it. Jerez obtained DO status in 1933, and the management of flor and solera became increasingly formalised. Sherry consumption rose and fell in the late twentieth century, but Fino and Manzanilla remained part of everyday Spanish eating and drinking.

Terroir and varieties

The heart of the region is the "Sherry triangle" of Jerez de la Frontera, El Puerto de Santa María and Sanlúcar de Barrameda. Summers are hot and winters mild, with Atlantic and Mediterranean influences alternating. The humid poniente wind from the Atlantic and the dry, hot levante from the interior shape ripening.

Albariza, a white chalky soil, is the most famous. It is shallow and retains water well, helping vines through the dry summer. The main variety is Palomino (Palomino Fino), low in acidity and neutral in aroma, which makes it ideal for a wine whose character will be driven by flor or oxygen. Sweet styles use Pedro Ximénez and Moscatel; PX grapes are sun-dried to concentrate sugar, giving raisin and fig flavours.

Base wine and fortification

Grapes are pressed and fermented. The finished base wine is usually 11%–13% ABV, dry and modest in acidity. The bodega then classifies it: light, clean wines are chosen for flor, while fuller, deeper wines go towards oxidative ageing.

Fortification uses grape spirit. Fino and Manzanilla are raised to about 15% ABV, strong enough to inhibit acetic bacteria but low enough for flor yeast to survive. Oloroso is raised to 17%–18%, which prevents flor and sends the wine into oxidative ageing. Amontillado follows a more complex route: it begins as a Fino under flor, then is fortified further and moved to oxidative ageing after the flor dies, so it carries both bread and nut aromas.

Biological and oxidative ageing

The key to biological ageing is flor. Flor is a film of yeast floating on the wine surface. It consumes alcohol and oxygen and produces acetaldehyde and some esters, giving Fino its bread, almond, green apple and saline notes while shielding the wine from excessive oxidation. Flor growth depends on temperature, humidity, strength and nutrients. Sanlúcar is cooler and more humid, so the film is thicker and Manzanilla tastes more obviously of the sea.

Oxidative ageing lets the wine meet oxygen. Colour moves from pale yellow to amber and mahogany, and aroma turns to walnut, hazelnut, caramel, orange peel and spice. Oloroso, Amontillado, Palo Cortado and sweet PX all follow this path. Biological and oxidative ageing are two directions, not two grades.

The solera system

The other core of Sherry is the solera. Casks are stacked in tiers. The bottom tier, the solera, holds the oldest wine ready for bottling; the tiers above, the criaderas, hold progressively younger wine. When wine is drawn from the solera, an equal volume is replaced from the next tier up, and so on, with new wine entering the top.

Because wine is drawn and replenished in stages, the finished product has no vintage and the style remains stable over time. A solera is not a casual blend of different years; it is a regulated system of tiers, records and fixed withdrawal ratios. The regulations also provide VOS (at least 20 years' average age) and VORS (at least 30 years) designations for long-aged Sherry.

Categories

By ageing path and sweetness, Sherry falls into several groups:

  • Fino: biological ageing, bone dry, light, with bread, almond and saline notes.
  • Manzanilla: a Fino-style wine from Sanlúcar, with a thicker flor and more obvious saltiness.
  • Amontillado: biological ageing followed by oxidative ageing; amber, dry, with hazelnut and orange peel.
  • Oloroso: oxidative ageing; dark, dry, with walnut, caramel and spice.
  • Palo Cortado: a rare intermediate style, combining Amontillado finesse with Oloroso weight.
  • Pedro Ximénez (PX): sweet, made from sun-dried grapes, with raisin, fig and syrup.
  • Moscatel: sweet, floral and candied.
  • Cream: Oloroso sweetened with sweet wine or PX.
  • Medium / Pale Cream: Amontillado or Fino based and sweetened, sitting between dry and Cream.

Sensory profile

Fino is pale straw in colour, with bread, almond, green apple, sea salt and yeast. It is dry and light, with a clean finish; Manzanilla adds more salt and mineral. Amontillado is darker, moving from bread to hazelnut, orange peel and tobacco while remaining dry. Oloroso is mahogany, with walnut, caramel, coffee and spice, and a fuller body. PX is deep brown, with raisin, fig, syrup and coffee, high in sugar but held together by acidity.

Sweetness and colour do not map neatly onto each other. Amontillado can be dry yet fairly dark, and Cream is dark and sweet. Read the category word, not just the colour.

Regions

The DO covers Jerez de la Frontera, El Puerto de Santa María, Sanlúcar de Barrameda and neighbouring municipalities. Jerez is the administrative and bodega centre; El Puerto was historically the export port; Sanlúcar is known for Manzanilla and has a cooler, more humid climate.

Distance from the coast, soil and the microclimate of individual cellars all affect flor and maturation. Treating Sherry as a single style ignores the gap between Fino and Oloroso, and the subtler differences between Jerez and Sanlúcar.

Representative products

Representative bottlings are best understood by type: classic Fino brands such as Tío Pepe; Manzanilla from Sanlúcar houses such as La Gitana; long-aged Amontillado and Oloroso from Lustau, Osborne and Barbadillo; and PX bottled on its own or used to sweeten Cream.

These names are style reference points, not a quality ranking. Look at the category, strength, ageing designation and sweetness rather than the fame of the house.

Tasting and serving

Serve Fino and Manzanilla well chilled at 6–8°C and drink them soon after opening, before oxygen takes hold. Amontillado and Oloroso are better at 12–15°C and can be given a little air. PX and Cream can be served cool or at room temperature in small glasses. Use a tulip glass or a copita; smell first, then judge dryness, body and finish.

With food, Fino suits olives, almonds, seafood and ham; Manzanilla suits salty snacks and fried food; Amontillado suits soups, mushrooms and poultry; Oloroso suits red meat, game and aged cheese; PX suits blue cheese, chocolate and desserts. Excessive drinking is harmful, and minors should not drink.

Common misconceptions

First, that all Sherry is sweet. Fino, Manzanilla, Amontillado and Oloroso can all be bone dry. Second, that Sherry is only an after-dinner drink. Fino is a classic aperitif in Spain. Third, that a "Sherry cask" whisky contains Sherry. The cask once held Sherry; the whisky does not. Fourth, that a solera has a vintage. It is a fractional blending system with no single year. Fifth, that darker means sweeter. Amontillado can be dark and dry. Sixth, that opened Sherry keeps like ordinary wine. Fino in particular is fragile and should be finished quickly.
