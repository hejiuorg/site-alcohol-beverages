Rice

The grain left after rice is husked and milled, and an important starch source for baijiu, yellow wine and sake; japonica and indica rice differ in starch structure, stickiness and steaming behaviour, which directly affect saccharification and fermentation.

Rice is the grain left after paddy is husked and milled, and belongs to the genus Oryza in the grass family. It is a staple across Asia and an important starch source for baijiu, yellow wine and sake. Unlike wheat and sorghum, rice has less protein and fat, its starch granules are fine, and it becomes sticky after cooking; these traits shape how it behaves in saccharification and fermentation.

Figure: Rice. Image: China News Service, CC BY 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Definition and forms

By starch structure, the rice eaten daily falls mainly into japonica and indica types; glutinous rice, with a very high proportion of amylopectin and strong stickiness, is usually discussed separately. Japonica grains are shorter with moderate stickiness, while indica grains are longer and less sticky. Brewing rice is judged by total starch, grain integrity, protein and broken-kernel ratio, not by "whiteness" alone.

The degree of milling changes how much of the outer layers remains. The outer grain contains protein, fat and ash; the whiter the rice, the higher the starch proportion, but vitamins and flavour compounds also decrease. Sake controls the milling ratio through the polishing percentage, while baijiu and yellow wine choose different grades of rice according to process and cost.

Main producing regions

Rice production is concentrated in Asia, with China, India, Indonesia, Bangladesh, Vietnam and Thailand among the important producers. Both southern and north-eastern China grow rice, and climate, variety and cultivation affect starch structure and moisture. Rice from different regions differs in water uptake during steaming, stickiness and saccharification behaviour.

The same variety can also differ by year because of temperature and rainfall. Drying and storage after harvest matter as well; high moisture, mould or pests reduce raw material safety and bring off-flavours. Large-scale production usually tests incoming rice for moisture, impurities and mouldy kernels.

Role in baijiu

In baijiu, rice can be a single-grain raw material or part of a multi-grain recipe. Strong-aroma baijiu commonly combines sorghum, rice, glutinous rice, wheat and maize; rice brings softness and a clean, refreshing quality. Some small-qu baijiu uses rice as the main raw material together with small qu for saccharification and fermentation.

Rice in baijiu usually needs steaming to gelatinise the starch so that enzymes can act. Over-steaming makes the grain sticky and clumpy and reduces air permeability, while under-steaming leaves starch poorly used. Solid-state fermentation demands a loose, breathable grain bed, so rice is often paired with sorghum and rice husks rather than carrying all the starch alone.

Role in yellow wine and sake

Yellow wine uses rice as its main raw material, and glutinous rice, japonica rice and ordinary rice may all appear. Glutinous rice brings stickiness and fullness, japonica rice provides more stable fermentation, and different ratios create different styles. Wheat qu or wine starter handles saccharification, converting rice starch into fermentable sugar before alcoholic fermentation.

Sake is also built around rice, but emphasises polishing, steaming and koji. Sake first uses koji to break down steamed rice starch into sugar, then ferments it together with yeast in a parallel process; a higher polishing ratio removes more outer protein and fat and usually gives a cleaner aroma. Sake rice varieties and cultivation requirements are not identical to table rice.

Starch, stickiness and process fit

Rice starch contains both amylopectin and amylose in proportions that differ between japonica and indica types. Rice high in amylopectin becomes very sticky after cooking and tends to clump during saccharification; rice higher in amylose has a looser structure but may lack fullness. Processes control these differences through steeping, steaming time, cooling and turning.

Rice protein and fat are relatively low, so fermentation produces fewer off-flavours, but "purer is better" is not a rule. Excessive polishing loses some flavour precursors and raises cost, while retaining some outer material can bring richer aroma. Milling degree, steaming behaviour and the target drink should be judged together.

Buying and storage

When buying rice, look at grain integrity, broken-kernel ratio, colour and smell. Brewing rice should be fresh, free of musty odour, pests and impurities. Store it away from moisture, insects and light, and keep it apart from strong-smelling items; hot and humid conditions speed quality loss and mould.

Home brewers should choose rice of clear origin and a recent production date, and pay attention to drinking water and equipment hygiene. Large-scale production also checks pesticide residues and heavy metals; these safety items should not be replaced by "good taste".

Common misconceptions

First, that rice is only a staple food. It is a core raw material in baijiu, yellow wine and sake.

Second, that whiter rice is better for brewing. Over-milling loses flavour precursors and raises cost.

Third, that all rice can be swapped freely. Japonica, indica and glutinous rice differ in starch structure and steaming behaviour.

Fourth, that sake rice is ordinary table rice. Sake has dedicated rice varieties and polishing requirements.

Fifth, that rice liquor must be bland. Flavour depends on qu, fermentation, distillation and ageing, not on one raw material alone.
