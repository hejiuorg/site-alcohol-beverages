LVMH

LVMH (Moët Hennessy Louis Vuitton) is a French luxury group formed in 1987 by the merger of Moët Hennessy and Louis Vuitton, headquartered in Paris. Its drinks business, run by the Moët Hennessy subsidiary, covers Champagne, cognac, wine and whisky. It is not a "distillery" but a holding and operating platform made up of many regions, brands and legal entities; the key is to separate the group, the drinks division and an individual brand.

Group identity and listing

The group is listed on Euronext Paris, with businesses in fashion and leather goods, wines and spirits, perfume and cosmetics, watches and jewellery, and selective retailing. Drinks are only one part, accounting for 7% of group sales in 2025, so group revenue cannot be read as the size of the drinks business.

The headquarters are in Paris, but drinks production is spread across Champagne, Cognac, Bordeaux and Burgundy in France, as well as Scotland, New Zealand and Yunnan in China. For a given product, state where the key steps take place rather than generalising from the headquarters.

The 1987 merger and family control

In 1987 Moët Hennessy and Louis Vuitton merged to form LVMH. The aim was to combine drinks cash flow with leather-goods luxury; a series of acquisitions later extended the brand portfolio.

On control, Bernard Arnault and his family hold a significant stake through holding companies and have long supplied the chairman and CEO. Public material uses different definitions for the family's combined shareholding and voting power, so this producer does not state a specific percentage. Family control is a long-term feature of governance.

The drinks business within the group

Drinks are operated by Moët Hennessy and cover Champagne, wine, cognac, vodka, whisky and other spirits. In 2025, wines and spirits revenue was EUR 5.358 billion, down 5% organic and 9% reported; profit from recurring operations was EUR 1.016 billion, down 25%.

Two points follow: drinks are a small share of the group but important for brand image and channel relationships; and the demand normalisation that began in 2023 is still running, with Champagne and cognac most exposed. Treating drinks as the group's growth engine is outdated.

Moët Hennessy and Diageo's 34%

Moët Hennessy is not wholly owned by LVMH. Diageo holds 34% of Moët Hennessy through two legal entities, and LVMH holds the remaining 66%. The structure dates from the 1990s and continues today.

For LVMH, begin with the range it makes. Then look at the site and the owner, and finally ask the bottler named on the label. The answers point to different choices, not to a single score.

The Champagne portfolio

Champagne is the image core. The portfolio includes Moët & Chandon, Dom Pérignon, Veuve Clicquot, Krug, Ruinart and Mercier, covering several price tiers from entry to premium. In 2025 the group's Champagne houses together maintained more than 22% of Champagne-appellation shipments.

Champagne is based on secondary fermentation in the bottle and lees ageing; brands differ in lees contact, dosage and base-wine structure, producing a spectrum from fresh to rich. Krug is known for oak fermentation and long ageing and is one of the portfolio's most distinctive premium houses.

Cognac and spirits

Cognac centres on Hennessy, an important source of drinks revenue. In 2025 Hennessy was held back by weaker demand in China and the United States. Cognac relies on copper pot distillation and oak ageing, with blending central to consistency.

The spirits portfolio also includes vodka (Belvedere) and Scotch whisky (Glenmorangie, Ardbeg). Regulations, raw materials and occasions differ greatly, and one group must run them with different product structures and channels.

Wine and estates

The wine portfolio includes Château d'Yquem in Bordeaux, Cheval Blanc in Saint-Émilion, Clos des Lambrays in Burgundy, Cloudy Bay in New Zealand and Ao Yun in Yunnan, China. These estates cover sweet wine, red wine and still white wine beyond Champagne and sparkling.

The estate logic differs from Champagne and cognac: it depends more on vintage, terroir and yield control, and the brand narrative is built around plots and years. Treating estate wine as mass-market wine misreads price and scarcity.

2025 performance and market pressure

In 2025 group revenue was EUR 80.8 billion; drinks revenue was EUR 5.358 billion, or 7%; Champagne and wine were 58% of drinks and cognac and spirits 42%. Drinks profit from recurring operations fell 25%, more than revenue.

Pressure comes from weaker cognac demand in China and the United States, post-pandemic normalisation of Champagne, and tariffs and trade friction. The group's response is cost control and protection of brand investment, not deep discounting.

Channels, travel retail and capital market

Drinks sales rely on restaurants, nightlife, specialist retail, travel retail and e-commerce. Travel retail matters especially for cognac, where airport and duty-free traffic directly affects volume. Champagne depends more on festivals, gifting and premium dining.

On the capital market, the group trades on Euronext Paris under "MC". Investors buy the whole luxury group, not Moët Hennessy alone; drinks results are disclosed separately in group reports but not separately listed.

Governance and controversy

Controversy centres on three areas: slower drinks demand and the inventory cycle; tariffs and trade friction affecting cognac exports; and the balance between family control and minority shareholder rights. Holding companies and dual voting rights preserve control, limiting outside influence.

Public material does not disclose the full basis for the family's combined shareholding and voting power, and this producer does not fill it in. Specific equity and compensation matters should be checked against registration documents and shareholder meeting material.

Division of labour with the brand page

This producer covers the group and drinks division: who owns, who operates, how revenue is structured and how ownership works. Brand positioning, product lines and flavour belong on brand pages. Turning the group page into a brand catalogue blurs the line between ownership and product choice.

Krug, for example, is a group brand, but its style, price and vintage performance cannot be inferred directly from group results. Reading the layers separately makes sense of both capital structure and the shelf.

Common misconceptions

First, treating LVMH's drinks business as a Champagne house. Moët Hennessy also owns Cognac through Hennessy, Scotch through Glenmorangie and Ardbeg, vodka through Belvedere, and estates in New Zealand, Australia, Argentina and Spain.

Second, assuming luxury positioning sets a floor under every cuvée. Entry wines and spirits sit beside prestige cuvées; quality, price and vintage performance vary by product and market.

Third, overlooking the shareholder split. Moët Hennessy is 66% LVMH and 34% Diageo, so consolidated group accounts are not the same as day-to-day winemaking or brand ownership.

Fourth, equating Champagne with all sparkling wine. Champagne is a product of a specific region and process.
