Château d'Yquem

Château d'Yquem lies in Sauternes, Bordeaux, and is known for botrytised sweet white wine; it was rated Premier Cru Supérieur in the 1855 classification. Controlled by LVMH since 1999, it is made from Sémillon and Sauvignon Blanc, commonly 375 mL or 750 mL at about 13-14% vol.

Definition and position

The wine belongs to white wine and is the sweet branch of wine. Its place in Sauternes and the Bordeaux classification is unusual: in the 1855 classification it was the only estate rated Premier Cru Supérieur.

Three levels need separating: wine is the broad category, Sauternes botrytised sweet wine is a region and process type, and Yquem is a specific estate and product. Vintages from one estate differ greatly, and the brand name cannot be treated as a style description.

Producer and brand

The wine is made by an estate controlled by LVMH in Sauternes, Bordeaux. The estate's history goes back several centuries, and the ownership change in 1999 brought it into a luxury group.

Company, brand and product are three levels: the brand answers "what is it called and who is it for", the company answers "who owns the trademark and who organises production and distribution", and the product answers vintage, format and picking pass. Treating group scale as a guarantee of single-bottle quality is a common confusion of levels.

Region and ingredients

Sauternes lies south of Bordeaux where the Garonne and Ciron meet; misty autumn mornings and sunny afternoons favour the growth of Botrytis cinerea. The fungus concentrates sugars and flavours as the grapes lose water and adds its own distinctive aromas.

The main varieties are Sémillon and Sauvignon Blanc. Sémillon supplies sugar, body and botrytis aroma, while Sauvignon Blanc supplies acidity and citrus notes. Variety proportions and picking passes are adjusted by vintage.

Production

Botrytised grapes are picked by hand in several passes, selecting only suitably affected fruit. After pressing the juice undergoes fermentation, usually in oak, followed by oak ageing, then blending and bottling.

Ageing time and the share of new oak shape aroma and structure. Sweet wines carry high residual sugar, and acidity is the key to balance; fermentation temperature and when it stops determine sweetness and body. Specific parameters are estate information, and labels usually disclose only part of it.

Specification and label

Common formats are 375 mL and 750 mL bottles at about 13-14% vol. The estate name, vintage, region, net volume and classification on the label are the basics for identifying a product.

Vintage matters especially for botrytised sweet wine: the degree of botrytis infection varies greatly by year and directly affects yield and style. Buyers should check vintage, batch and back label rather than treating a gift box as a grade.

Flavour and tasting

The aroma is led by apricot, peach, citrus, honey and the distinctive botrytis notes; the entry is very sweet but acidity supports it, the body is full and the finish long. With age come nuts, caramel and greater complexity.

Serve well chilled, about 8-10°C. A dessert-wine glass or a smaller white-wine glass concentrates aroma. Pour small amounts so the wine does not warm too quickly.

Drinking and pairing

Botrytised sweet wine suits blue cheese, fruit tarts, foie gras and desserts that are not too sweet. Match sweetness carefully: the wine should be sweeter than the dessert, or it will taste sour by contrast.

It can also be served alone as an after-dinner drink. After opening, refrigerate and finish soon; sweet wine oxidises more slowly, but the aroma still declines. Do not drink when driving, taking medication or pregnant.

Buying and storage

Buy through regular channels and check the estate name, vintage, back label and anti-counterfeit information. Prices differ markedly by vintage and format, while gift and ordinary packs differ mainly in packaging.

Unopened bottles should be stored away from light, in a cool place, lying down, avoiding heat and strong vibration. Home storage cannot replicate an estate's ageing conditions, so the year of purchase should not be equated simply with an improvement in quality.

Common misconceptions

First, treating botrytised sweet wine as sweetened wine. The sweetness comes from concentrated grapes, not added sugar.

Second, assuming sweet wine must pair with sweet desserts. A mismatch makes the wine taste sour.

Third, treating the brand name as a vintage guarantee. Vintage variation is especially large in botrytised sweet wine.

Fourth, treating the 1855 classification as a permanent quality ranking. It reflected prices and reputation at the time.

Fifth, judging authenticity by price or packaging. Channel, back label and anti-counterfeit features are more reliable.

Château d'Yquem is best approached through the base and the stated strength, then the sweetness or bitterness, and then how it is served. One check alone will not settle it.

White wine explains style; Bordeaux adds region and vintage. Put Château d'Yquem between them before deciding what it is.

At a glance: A botrytised sweet white wine from Sauternes in Bordeaux, rated Premier Cru Supérieur in the 1855 classification and controlled by LVMH since 1999; made from Sémillon and Sauvignon Blanc, commonly 375 mL or 750 mL at about 13-14% vol.

The usual confusion is to treat Château d'Yquem as a drink style or a brand. Check which style it belongs to first, then who makes it and which vintage or batch; the two need not agree.
