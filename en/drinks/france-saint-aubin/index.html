Saint-Aubin

A village-level appellation on the high ground of a Côte de Beaune valley, often hidden behind the ridge from Meursault and Chassagne-Montrachet. White wine dominates and red is also made; there is no grand cru, about thirty premiers crus, and the whites show high acidity and strong minerality, usually a more restrained Côte de Beaune white; commonly 750 mL at about 12.5%–13.5% vol.

Saint-Aubin's vineyards lie in a valley partly enclosed by a ridge: the village hides behind Meursault and Chassagne-Montrachet, and the slopes run from the valley floor up to high ground over limestone and clay. The ridge separates the village from the most famous white wine slope and leaves it with higher altitude, a later ripening rhythm and clearer acidity. The village name rarely appears in a prominent place on a wine list, and prices are usually more restrained than on the other side of the ridge, but it is not simply "the back of Meursault". Bottles are commonly 750 mL at about 12.5%–13.5% alcohol by volume; white wine is made from Chardonnay and red wine from Pinot Noir, with white holding both the larger output and the greater reputation.

The Côte de Beaune white hidden behind a ridge

Saint-Aubin lies in the Côte de Beaune of Burgundy, France. The village sits in a valley, separated by a ridge from Meursault and Chassagne-Montrachet to the east. The ridge blocks part of the sunlight and heat and also separates the village from the famous white wine slope, so the vineyards sit around the village and on the high ground behind the ridge.

This position shapes the village's market image: it is often treated as the back side of Meursault rather than as an appellation in its own right. In fact Saint-Aubin has its own village and premier cru system, and its labels carry only its own village name. Treating the two sides of the ridge as one appellation ignores the differences in altitude, gradient and ripening rhythm. The official delimitation governs the boundaries.

High slopes, limestone and about thirty premiers crus

The vineyards run from near the village up to higher ground. Altitude and gradient make ripening a little later and preserve acidity better; the soil is mainly limestone and clay, thin and stony upslope with fast drainage. Different aspects and altitudes produce differences in ripeness and body, and this is the main reason Saint-Aubin whites show such a clear acid line and such prominent minerality.

The village has about 30 premiers crus, a substantial number for a Côte de Beaune village, but most individual sites are small, fragmented and held by many owners. Common premiers crus include En Remilly, Les Murgers des Dents de Chien, Sur Gamay, Les Combes and La Chatenière. En Remilly sits high on the slope, not far from the famous hill that carries Chevalier-Montrachet, and is often compared with the whites of the great villages; Les Murgers des Dents de Chien takes its name from the limestone stones that break through the soil, and drains very well. Premier cru names must match the official list.

A village of white first, red second

Saint-Aubin makes both colours, but the whites are more systematic and more representative. White wine uses Chardonnay from grape, red uses Pinot Noir; the reds are often planted on cooler or slower-ripening parcels and are lighter in body, with red fruit and flowers to the fore. The whites gain higher acidity and a tighter texture from the high parcels.

The village has no grand cru and only two tiers, village and premier cru. That structure gives Saint-Aubin a different reputation path from its neighbours: it cannot rely on a grand cru to attract attention, so it builds its name on the differences between premiers crus and on consistent value. For drinkers this means looking more carefully at the site name and the producer rather than trusting one loud famous name.

Family estates and a reputation without a grand cru

Family estates dominate the village, with merchants and cooperatives also taking part. Some estates hold parcels in Saint-Aubin and in neighbouring famous villages, and the bottler information on the label is the key to telling estate wine from merchant wine. With no grand cru, estates here rely more on premiers crus and village wines to build reputation, and they are more inclined to name the specific site on the label.

Producer, appellation and product are three levels: the appellation answers for rules and parcel range, the producer answers for who farms, who makes and who bottles, and the product answers for vintage, tier and colour. The same Saint-Aubin can differ noticeably by slope position and estate, and the same premier cru can turn out differently under different owners according to picking date and winemaking.

Winemaking for early drinking: lighter extraction and lees

After harvest the red fruit is usually sorted, and some producers keep a proportion of whole clusters to add aromatic complexity. Fermentation runs in temperature-controlled vessels, where maceration time and temperature shape colour, tannin and aroma. Saint-Aubin reds usually aim for lighter extraction and earlier drinkability, so maceration is not especially long. Whites are usually pressed before fermentation, and some are stirred on their lees to add texture, though usually more gently than in Meursault, so as not to bury the high-slope minerality.

The wine then ages in oak, with the proportion of new barrels varying by estate and tier. Premiers crus may see more new oak and village wines less; for Saint-Aubin whites too much new oak easily wraps up the acidity and flint, so many estates choose a smaller share or larger, older barrels. Clarification and filtration before bottling depend on the producer.

Against Meursault and Chassagne-Montrachet

Typical Saint-Aubin whites show lemon, white flowers, green apple and flinty minerality, with higher acidity, a tight texture and a light-to-medium body. Compared with Meursault to the east, Saint-Aubin is leaner, more acidic and more mineral, while Meursault is rounder and more often shows hazelnut and cream. Compared with Chassagne-Montrachet, Saint-Aubin has less thickness and sweetness and more freshness and line. Put simply: Meursault is more opulent, Chassagne has more weight, and Saint-Aubin is tighter, more acidic and usually a step lower in price.

Typical reds show red cherry, raspberry and flowers, with earth and spice in some wines; the palate has clear acidity, fine tannin and a light-to-medium body, usually ready earlier. Serve reds at about 15–17 degrees Celsius and whites at about 10–12 degrees Celsius. Serving Saint-Aubin whites too cold turns the minerality into monotonous acidity, while too warm a serving makes them feel loose.

Warm and cool years: acidity and minerality

Warm years usually bring riper citrus and white flowers, a rounder body and slightly lower acidity, and the minerality can be covered by fruit; cool years retain higher acidity, with green apple, lemon peel and flint more prominent, a leaner body and a tighter line. The high slopes actually do better in warm years because cooler nights slow the loss of acidity, while warmer parcels at the foot can become broad in a warm season.

In rainy years, fast-draining parcels suffer less, while parcels with more clay may see dilution or uneven ripening. For buyers, cool-year Saint-Aubin needs food more, while warm-year bottles are easier to drink on their own; comparing one estate's warm and cool years says more about vintage than comparing two estates. Buying Côte de Beaune white on a premier cru budget

Village-level Saint-Aubin white usually sits in the entry to middle band of Côte de Beaune whites; premier cru moves up one step but still often costs less than Meursault or Chassagne-Montrachet at the same tier. Reds are usually cheaper still, because the reputation is concentrated in the whites. Judge by site name, bottler, vintage and back-label information rather than by the village name alone.

A lower price does not mean lower quality: Saint-Aubin's value often lies in buying a clear Côte de Beaune white for less. On a limited budget, the premiers crus En Remilly and Les Murgers des Dents de Chien are often worth checking first; for a fuller style, Meursault or Chassagne may still be the answer. No specific figures are given here. For Saint-Aubin, unopened bottles should be kept away from light, lying flat at about 12–15 degrees Celsius, avoiding heat and sharp temperature swings.

Four misconceptions that come with the back-hill label

First, treating Saint-Aubin as an appendage of Meursault. It is a separate village-level appellation with its own village and premier cru system. Second, assuming the village makes only white wine. The village appellation covers both colours, and reds are a real share, though less famous. Third, thinking no grand cru means no good wine. Tier is not quality, and some premiers crus are standout sites; En Remilly is a much-discussed example. Fourth, equating high slopes with necessarily higher acidity. Vintage, aspect and ripeness vary greatly, so no blanket rule applies.
