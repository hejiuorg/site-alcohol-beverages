Hautes-Côtes de Nuits

A Burgundy appellation in the hills behind the Côte de Nuits, France, with vineyards on the slopes above the Côte de Nuits villages; red wine is made from Pinot Noir and white wine from Chardonnay, and the higher altitude and later ripening give a style known for fresh acidity and earlier drinking, commonly 750 mL at about 11.5%–13% vol.

Hautes-Côtes de Nuits is a Burgundy appellation in the hills behind the Côte de Nuits, France, with vineyards on the slopes above the Côte de Nuits villages. The appellation makes red wine from Pinot Noir and white wine from Chardonnay. It is commonly 750 mL at about 11.5%–13% alcohol by volume.

Definition and position

Start with the levels: red wine and white wine are the categories, Pinot Noir and Chardonnay are the varieties, Hautes-Côtes de Nuits is the appellation (AOC), and individual estates and wines are the producer level. Hautes-Côtes de Nuits is a separate appellation from the main Côte de Nuits slope, and labels usually read Hautes-Côtes de Nuits or Bourgogne Hautes-Côtes de Nuits.

The appellation has no grands crus and no premiers crus; all wines carry a regional appellation with a specific place name. It is a different legal identity from the village appellations on the main slope, such as Nuits-Saint-Georges, and from the collective Côte de Nuits-Villages, and the tiers must not be mixed.

Producer and brand

The appellation spans several villages, and estate sizes vary widely, with négociants and cooperatives also involved. Because the vineyards are scattered across the hills behind the Côte de Nuits, a merchant may buy grapes or wine from different villages and bottle it. The bottler information on the label is the key to telling estate wine from merchant wine.

Producer, appellation and product are three levels: the appellation answers for rules and parcel range, the producer answers for who farms, who makes and who bottles, and the product answers for vintage, village sources and red or white type. Even within Hautes-Côtes de Nuits, different villages and estates can differ.

Region and ingredients

Hautes-Côtes de Nuits lies in the higher hills west of the main Côte de Nuits slope in Burgundy, France, with vineyards on the slopes above villages including Nuits-Saint-Georges, Vosne-Romanée, Chambolle-Musigny and Gevrey-Chambertin. Altitude is usually higher than on the main slope, aspects vary, and the overall climate is cooler.

The higher altitude delays ripening, keeps more acidity and makes vintage differences more pronounced. Soils are mainly limestone and marl, with gravel and clay mixed in; different villages and aspects produce differences in ripeness and body. The official delimitation governs the boundaries.

The raw material is grape. Red wine uses Pinot Noir and white wine uses Chardonnay. The cooler setting pushes Pinot Noir towards red fruit and flowers, while Chardonnay keeps higher acidity and fresh citrus notes.

Winemaking

For Hautes-Côtes de Nuits, red fruit is usually sorted after harvest, and some producers keep a proportion of whole clusters to add aromatic complexity. Fermentation runs in temperature-controlled vessels, where maceration time and temperature shape colour, tannin and aroma; wines made for early drinking usually see shorter maceration and lighter extraction. White fruit is usually pressed before fermentation, and some wines are stirred on the lees to add texture.

The wine then ages in oak, usually with a modest proportion of new barrels, to keep the fruit and the fresh acidity. Oak serves to integrate aroma and soften tannin rather than mask the variety's delicacy. Clarification and filtration before bottling depend on the producer.

Specification and label

The common volume is 750 mL, with alcohol by volume at about 11.5%–13% vol, varying by vintage, village and red or white type. The label should be checked for the appellation name, red or white type, vintage, bottler and importer; some back labels name the source village, but that is not part of the legal appellation name.

Hautes-Côtes de Nuits carries no grand cru or premier cru designation, so if a label shows "Premier Cru" or "Grand Cru", check whether it actually belongs to another appellation. The Chinese back label, importer and anti-counterfeit information are the basis for checking identity; label languages differ by market, so the bottle on hand governs.

Flavour and tasting

Typical red aromas are red cherry, raspberry, strawberry and flowers, with earth and spice in some wines; the palate has higher acidity, relatively fine tannin and a light-to-medium body, usually fresher and earlier-drinking than the village wines of the main Côte de Nuits slope. With age it can develop leaf litter and mushroom notes.

White wines often show lemon, green apple, white flowers and flinty minerality, with high acidity and a fresh texture. Serve reds at about 14–16 degrees Celsius and whites at about 10–12 degrees Celsius; too warm turns freshness into thinness.

Drinking and pairing

For Hautes-Côtes de Nuits, reds suit poultry, roasted pork, mushrooms, sausages and medium-strength cheese; whites suit steamed fish, shellfish, poultry and soft cheese. Avoid very hot or sharply acidic dishes, which mask the fruit and delicate aromas.

Most wines are for drinking within 3–6 years and are not for long cellaring; the drinking window for whites is shorter. After opening, refrigerate and finish within a few days. Excessive drinking harms health and minors should not drink.

Buying and storage

Buy from reputable channels and check the appellation name, red or white type, vintage and bottler information. Hautes-Côtes de Nuits is usually positioned as an entry to the Côte de Nuits, and prices vary widely with village source, estate and vintage, so no figure is given here. In higher-altitude, later-ripening years, ripeness deserves extra attention.

For Hautes-Côtes de Nuits, unopened bottles should be kept away from light, lying flat in a cool place at an ideal 12–15 degrees Celsius, avoiding heat and sharp temperature swings. Cork-sealed bottles need humidity and can be stored lying down. After opening, use a vacuum or inert gas and refrigerate rather than warming and cooling repeatedly.

Common misconceptions

First, treating Hautes-Côtes de Nuits as part of the main Côte de Nuits slope. It is a separate appellation in the hills behind it. Second, assuming it has grands crus or premiers crus. The appellation carries only a regional designation with a specific place name. Third, conflating Hautes-Côtes de Nuits with Hautes-Côtes de Beaune. They lie in different parts of the Côte de Nuits and Côte de Beaune. Fourth, assuming higher altitude always means more acidity. Vintage and ripeness vary greatly, so no blanket rule applies. Fifth, judging authenticity by price or packaging. Channel, label and bottler information are more reliable.

Hautes-Côtes de Nuits is best approached through the base and the stated strength, then the sweetness or bitterness, and then how it is served. One check alone will not settle it.
