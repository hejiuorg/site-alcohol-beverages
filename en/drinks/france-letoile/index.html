L'Étoile is a small white-wine appellation in northern Jura, France, making only white wine as well as vin jaune and vin de paille. The varieties are mainly Chardonnay and Savagnin, and the style is fresh with high acidity and clear minerality. It is commonly 750 mL at about 12.5%–14.5% alcohol by volume.

Figure: L'Étoile. Project-generated or category illustration used for wine category context; not a photograph of the subject.

Definition and position

Start with the levels: white wine is the type, Chardonnay and Savagnin are the varieties, L'Étoile is the appellation, and individual estates and wines are the producer level. L'Étoile is both a village name and an appellation name, and the label usually reads "L'Étoile" directly.

L'Étoile has a special position: the appellation allows only white wine, vin jaune and vin de paille, with no red wine. Its style differences therefore come mainly from parcel, vintage and whether the wine follows the sous voile route rather than from the choice of colour. The name is often said to come from star-shaped fossils in the local limestone.

Producer and brand

The appellation is small, and producers are mostly family estates, with a few cooperatives and merchants. Because volumes are limited, one estate often makes standard white, vin jaune and vin de paille at the same time.

Producer, appellation and product are three levels: the appellation answers for rules and area, the producer answers for farming, making and bottling, and the product answers for vintage, wine type and bottle format. The same L'Étoile can differ between estates in oxidative management and ageing length.

Region and ingredients

L'Étoile lies in northern Jura, with vineyards on slopes around the village at about 250–400 metres. Soils are mainly limestone and marl, with some parcels containing scree, well drained and low in fertility.

Aspect and altitude produce differences in ripeness: south-facing slopes are warmer, and higher parcels keep better acidity. The raw material is grape. White grapes are mainly Chardonnay and Savagnin; Chardonnay brings citrus and white flowers, while Savagnin carries nut and spice potential.

Winemaking

Standard whites mostly follow the ouillé topped-up route: the barrel is regularly filled to replace evaporating wine, reducing oxygen contact and giving a pure citrus, white-flower and mineral style. Vin jaune follows the sous voile route: the barrel is not topped up, a yeast film forms on the surface, and the wine evolves over a long period in an oxidative environment.

Fermentation temperature and the use of oak shape texture and aroma. Some wines ferment in stainless steel to emphasise acidity and purity, while others use old barrels to add roundness. See fermentation for the basic process.

Specification and label

The common volume is 750 mL, with alcohol by volume at about 12.5%–14.5% vol; vin jaune uses a 620 mL narrow-necked bottle. The label should be checked for the L'Étoile appellation, vintage, wine type and bottler.

"Vin jaune" marks the oxidative yellow wine and "vin de paille" the straw wine; a label reading only "L'Étoile" without a wine type is usually a standard white. The bottle on hand governs.

Flavour and tasting

Standard whites show lemon, green apple, white flowers, quince and mineral notes with high acidity, a light-to-medium body and a fresh finish. Vin jaune shows walnut, beeswax, curry and saline notes with a more concentrated palate.

Serve standard whites at about 10–12 degrees Celsius in a tulip-shaped or Burgundy glass; serve vin jaune at about 12–14 degrees Celsius in a small glass and in modest quantities. Distinguish the two styles in the same appellation, and avoid treating oxidative notes as a universal feature.

Drinking and pairing

Standard whites suit seafood, soft cheese, poultry and light dishes; vin jaune suits nuts, mushrooms, foie gras and hard cheese. Avoid dishes that are too sweet or too hot, which mask the detail.

Refrigerate after opening and finish soon; vin jaune oxidises quickly and should be drunk even sooner. Excessive drinking harms health and minors should not drink.

Buying and storage

First establish the wine type, then check appellation, vintage and bottler. L'Étoile is small with limited volume, so prices are usually higher than standard Côtes du Jura whites; no figures are given here.

For L'Étoile, unopened bottles should be kept away from light, lying flat in a cool place at an ideal 12–15 degrees Celsius. Vin jaune and vin de paille are more sensitive to light and heat, so long storage needs stable temperature. After opening, use a vacuum or inert gas and refrigerate.

Common misconceptions

First, treating L'Étoile as a red-wine appellation. It makes only white wine, vin jaune and vin de paille. Second, assuming the name comes from an estate brand. It comes from the place name and local fossils. Third, treating vin jaune as a sweet wine. "Jaune" refers to colour and oxidative style, not high sugar. Fourth, assuming a small appellation has a single style. Parcel and estate differences remain clear. Fifth, judging quality by price alone. Wine type, vintage and bottler information is more specific.

Short definition: A small white-wine appellation in northern Jura, France, making only white wine as well as vin jaune and vin de paille; the varieties are mainly Chardonnay and Savagnin, the soils mainly limestone and marl, and the style is fresh with high acidity and clear minerality, commonly 750 mL at about 12.5%–14.5% vol.

L'Étoile is best approached through the base and the stated strength, then the sweetness or bitterness, and then how it is served. One check alone will not settle it.
