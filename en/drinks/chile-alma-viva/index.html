Almaviva

Almaviva is a Bordeaux-style red wine from Puente Alto in Chile's Maipo Valley, founded in 1997 as a joint venture between Viña Concha y Toro and Baron Philippe de Rothschild SA. The first vintage was 1997 and was launched in 1998. It is led by Cabernet Sauvignon, with Carmenère, Cabernet Franc, Merlot and Petit Verdot in support, usually 750 mL at about 14% ABV.

1997: A Transatlantic Joint Venture

In 1997 Baroness Philippine de Rothschild, chairman of the advisory board of Baron Philippe de Rothschild SA, and Eduardo Guilisasti Tagle, chairman of Viña Concha y Toro, signed a partnership to create an exceptional Franco-Chilean wine. The project did not transplant a Mouton recipe to Chile; the two partners selected a site and built a vineyard and winery under joint technical supervision. The house describes itself as Chile's first wine made under the French “Château” concept: one exceptional terroir, one cellar and one dedicated team, all serving a single grand vin.

The name has an unusual origin. “Almaviva” sounds Spanish, but it comes from French classical literature: Count Almaviva is the hero of Beaumarchais's play The Marriage of Figaro. The label uses Beaumarchais's own handwriting, a detail that ties the French lineage to the Chilean site.

Puente Alto: Sixty-Five Hectares at the Top of the Maipo

The vineyard lies in Puente Alto, the highest and northernmost part of the Maipo Valley, on the north bank of the Maipo River at about 650 metres. It covers 65 hectares, and the oldest vines date from 1978. The site is cooler than the rest of the valley; cold air from the Andes and a wide day–night temperature swing slow ripening, lift acidity and concentrate colour and aroma. The winery calls it one of Chile's best sites for Cabernet Sauvignon.

Andean Alluvium and 300 Millimetres of Rain

The soil is Andean alluvium: about 50 centimetres of loamy clay, then sandy loam, gravel and rocks. Drainage is fast, fertility is low and pH is on the low side, so the vines root deeply and yields stay naturally modest. The climate is semi-arid Mediterranean, with about 300 millimetres of rain a year, mostly in winter. The growing season is almost dry, which keeps fungal pressure low but makes irrigation essential and makes each vintage's rainfall pattern visible in the wine.

The EPU 2022 technical sheet gives an extreme example. From May to September 2021 Puente Alto received only 70 millimetres of rain, 71% less than the 243 millimetres averaged over 1996–2021, and the vintage ended with just 79 millimetres for the year. Such conditions produce smaller, more concentrated berries, and make irrigation management decisive.

Five Bordeaux Varieties, Rearranged in Chile

The vineyard is planted with Cabernet Sauvignon, Carmenère, Cabernet Franc, Merlot and Petit Verdot, with Cabernet Sauvignon by far the most cultivated. The 2024 blend is 70% Cabernet Sauvignon, 24% Carmenère, 4% Cabernet Franc and 2% Petit Verdot; 2023 is 74%, 19%, 5% and 2%. The proportions move with the year. Carmenère can take a larger share in warm vintages, adding spice, dark chocolate and softer tannin.

Eighteen Months in French Oak, 76% New

The 2024 wine was aged for 18 months in French oak, 76% of it new; the 2023 vintage spent 20 months in 73% new oak. A high share of new wood brings vanilla, toast and sweet spice, and demands fully ripe fruit or the oak will dominate. Fruit is hand-harvested and sorted, then fermented parcel by parcel and variety by variety before blending.

EPU: A Second Wine With Its Own Job

EPU is Almaviva's second wine. The 2022 is 84% Cabernet Sauvignon, 10% Carmenère, 3% Merlot, 2% Cabernet Franc and 1% Petit Verdot, aged 12 months in French oak with only 10% new barrels. It shares the vineyard and the team with the grand vin, but the fruit selection, the time in barrel and the new-oak share are all lower, producing an earlier-drinking, more fruit-forward style. A second wine is not a failed grand vin; it is a different product with a different job.

Why It Shows More Spice Than Opus One

Compared with Opus One, Almaviva uses far more Carmenère (19%–24% versus 0%), sits higher at about 650 metres and ripens in a cooler site. Its aromatics therefore tend towards black pepper, bay leaf, dark chocolate and tobacco, with rounder tannin. Opus One, from warmer Oakville and a Cabernet-dominant blend, leans more towards graphite, cedar and vanilla. Both carry Rothschild influence, but terroir and varieties pull them apart.

Price Bands: Almaviva and EPU

Wine-Searcher put the average retail price of the grand vin at about CNY 1,233 per 750 mL in November 2026, with a common range of CNY 730–1,400, roughly US$100–200. EPU sits lower, typically at about half the price of the grand vin. Prices move with vintage, channel and exchange rate, and older vintages or magnums command more.

The Misconception: Not a Chilean Opus One

The most common misunderstanding is that Almaviva is a “Chilean Opus One”. Both have Rothschild involvement, but Almaviva was founded in 1997 with its first vintage in 1997, while Opus One was founded in 1979 with its first vintage in 1979. Almaviva grows Carmenère on Andean alluvium in Puente Alto; Opus One grows Cabernet Sauvignon in Oakville's To Kalon. Chilean and US regulations, variety proportions and ageing parameters all differ. A shared investor does not make the same wine.

Chilean Labels and the Chinese Back Label

The front label should show the brand, vintage, origin (Puente Alto / Maipo Valley), variety or blend information, alcohol, volume and producer. Chilean rules govern variety and origin claims, and the exact wording is best read from the bottle. For wine imported into China, a Chinese back label must correspond to the original information; producer, bottling location, importer and batch are more reliable than marketing copy.

Drinking, Decanting and Pairing

Serve at 16–18°C. Decant young vintages for one to two hours and give older bottles less air. Carmenère's rounder tannin means young bottles do not need excessively long decanting. Pair with steak, roast lamb, duck breast, hard cheese and spiced stews; the Carmenère spice works especially well with cumin, black pepper and smoked dishes. Recork, refrigerate and finish within a few days.
