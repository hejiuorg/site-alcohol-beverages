Penfolds Bin 389

Penfolds' classic Cabernet and Shiraz red, first made in 1960 and nicknamed Baby Grange because it matures in barrels that held the previous Grange vintage. The Bin number comes from a cellar storage bay, not a grade. Usually 750 mL and about 14.5% vol.

In the Chinese market the three digits "389" have almost become a brand of their own. Some drinkers treat it as Penfolds' second flagship, some as "Baby Grange", and some assume 389 is a score or a recipe number. None of these readings is entirely wrong, but none is precise. Bin 389 is a mid-to-upper red produced since 1960 from Cabernet Sauvignon and Shiraz, matured in barrels that previously held Grange; the number is only a cellar location.

One number, three misreadings

Three layers need separating. Penfolds is the winery and brand, Bin 389 is the product, and Cabernet Sauvignon and Shiraz are grape varieties. The word "Bin" comes from the numbered bays in a cellar where wine samples and barrels were kept; nineteenth- and twentieth-century wineries used them to distinguish batches and storage positions, and they later hardened into product codes.

So 389 is not a score, a grade, or "recipe number 389". Other wines in the same house, such as Bin 407, Bin 28 and Bin 150, each have their own numbering logic, and the size of the number does not track quality. Buying on the assumption that a larger number is better is the most common error.

1844: starting at Magill Estate

Penfolds was founded in 1844 by Christopher Penfold and his wife Mary near Magill in South Australia. The first plantings included grapes used for fortified and medicinal wines; by the end of the nineteenth century the estate had built a reputation for dry red wine. Magill Estate remains a historical symbol of the brand.

In the mid-twentieth century, chief winemaker Max Schubert travelled in Spain and France and decided to make an Australian red that could stand beside European classics. That wine became Grange. Experimental batches began in 1951 and were initially rejected by the market, yet Grange later became Australia's international wine symbol. Bin 389 is the downward-reaching version of that ambition.

1960 and the origin of Baby Grange

In 1960 Schubert made the first Bin 389. The winery wanted a family of wines with an obvious resemblance so that drinkers at different prices could taste the same stylistic DNA. Bin 389 therefore inherited part of Grange's oak, specifically the American oak barrels that had held the previous Grange vintage.

The nickname "Baby Grange" comes from that link. It describes a stylistic and barrel relationship, not an equal grade. Grange is built from a higher proportion of Cabernet Sauvignon, longer barrel maturation and stricter fruit selection, and its price and output are entirely different. Treating the nickname as a cheap substitute ignores the gap in structure and ageing potential.

Why Cabernet and Shiraz together

Cabernet Sauvignon supplies the skeleton: higher acidity, firm tannin, blackcurrant and cedar, and the capacity to age. Shiraz supplies flesh: blackberry, black pepper, body and fruit that is approachable earlier. Blending them is neither a simple compromise nor a slogan about combining strengths; it lets structure, fruit and acidity stand in one wine at the same time.

The blend has a long Australian tradition, usually called Cabernet Shiraz. Its advantage is clearest in regions with variable vintages: Cabernet can be lean in cool years, while Shiraz more reliably provides ripe fruit; in hot years Shiraz can become too full, and Cabernet's acidity pulls the wine back into balance.

Multi-region blending: South Australia's insurance policy

Bin 389 is not a single-vineyard wine but a multi-region blend. The 2021 vintage draws on McLaren Vale, Coonawarra, Barossa Valley, Wrattonbully and Padthaway. Coonawarra Cabernet supplies structure, Barossa and McLaren Vale Shiraz supply fruit and body, and Wrattonbully and Padthaway fill the middle palate and acidity.

The purpose is not to hide origin but to balance vintage variation and keep the style stable. Fruit from different regions is fermented separately and combined later; the winemaker decides proportions from each batch rather than following a fixed formula. This is one of the fundamental differences between a large house and a small grower: the former pursues predictable annual consistency, the latter accepts vintage variation.

Published parameters of the 2021 vintage

The published 2021 information is specific: 53% Cabernet Sauvignon and 47% Shiraz; fermentation, maceration, pressing and malolactic fermentation, then 12 months in 100% American oak hogsheads, 37% of them new; the Australian release is 750 mL with a cork closure, 14.5% vol, pH 3.67 and acidity 6.6 g/L.

These numbers say two things. First, the high share of American oak makes vanilla, coconut and sweet spice more obvious. Second, pH 3.67 and acidity 6.6 g/L are moderately high and sufficient to carry 14.5% vol and dense fruit. Parameters change by vintage, market and bottling, and the label always takes priority.

American oak: vanilla, coconut and softened tannin

American oak has a coarser grain and releases more vanillin and lactones after toasting, so it often brings vanilla, coconut, dill and sweet spice. It also softens tannin through micro-oxygenation, making a young wine easier to drink. French oak is more restrained and emphasises cedar, graphite and fine tannin.

Bin 389's style is shaped to a large extent by American oak: it gives the wine obvious sweet perfume and roundness when young, and makes the Baby Grange comparison tangible. But too much oak covers fruit, so the new-barrel share is held at 37% and the rest is older wood, adding complexity rather than turning the wine into a vanilla drink.

Flavour and drinking window

Typical notes are blackberry, blackcurrant, mocha and cedar, with fine, structured tannins, supporting acidity and a long finish. The published 2021 tasting description highlights black fruit, cocoa powder and savoury roast-meat notes, with fine tannins and well-integrated oak.

Serve at 16–18 degrees Celsius; young vintages can be decanted for about 1 hour, while older bottles need less decanting and careful handling of sediment. The published 2021 drinking window is 2025–2055, which indicates long cellaring capacity; actual condition still depends on temperature, humidity, transport and cork health. High alcohol increases the sensation of heat, especially with very spicy food.

Price band and collecting

Bin 389 sits in the mid-to-upper part of the Penfolds range: above everyday wines such as Bin 28 and Bin 407, below flagships such as Grange and Bin 707. Prices differ widely between markets because of duty, channel, vintage scores and exchange rates; the same case can vary by several tens of percent between retailers.

As a collecting object it belongs to the "can age, need not speculate" category. Vintage variation, storage condition and provenance matter more than short-term price movement. Check vintage, bottling version, importer label and anti-counterfeit information; older bottles on the secondary market without full provenance carry more risk than reward.

Common misconceptions

First, treating the Bin number as a score or grade. It is a cellar storage-bay number. Second, treating "Baby Grange" as an equal-grade product. The nickname only describes a stylistic and barrel link. Third, assuming the variety proportions are fixed. They change with vintage, region and the winemaker's judgement. Fourth, treating the vintage as time in bottle. The vintage is the grape harvest year, not the years since bottling. Fifth, assuming American oak means cheap. Barrel type is a stylistic choice, not a quality label. Sixth, using price instead of tasting. Vintage, storage condition and channel matter too.
