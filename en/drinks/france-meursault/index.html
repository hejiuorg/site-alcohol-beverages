Meursault

A dry white wine from the Meursault commune on Burgundy's Côte de Beaune in France, made from Chardonnay and known for premiers crus such as Les Perrières, Les Genevrières and Les Charmes. Fuller than Chablis, with hazelnut, butter, citrus and minerality; commonly 750 mL at about 12.5%–13.5% vol.

Meursault is a famous white-wine village on Burgundy's Côte de Beaune in France, its wines made from Chardonnay, with premiers crus such as Les Perrières, Les Genevrières and Les Charmes drawing the most attention. Compared with the lean style of Chablis, Meursault is usually fuller, with hazelnut, butter, citrus and minerality. It is commonly 750 mL at about 12.5%–13.5% alcohol by volume.

Figure: Meursault. Image: Benjamin Smith, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Definition and position

Start with the levels: white wine is the category, Chardonnay is the variety, Meursault is the appellation (AOC), and individual estates and wines are the producer level. Meursault is both a village name and an appellation name that may appear on a label, and neither should be confused with a brand.

Meursault is mainly white, but a small amount of red wine from Pinot Noir may also be made within the commune. On seeing "Meursault", first confirm whether it is white or red, village level or premier cru, and then judge style and price tier.

Producer and brand

There are many estates in Meursault, from small family domaines to famous houses holding several premiers crus, as well as négociants that buy grapes or wine and bottle it. The difference between estate wine and merchant wine usually shows in parcel control, picking standards and ageing.

Producer, appellation and product are three levels: the appellation answers for rules and parcel range, the producer answers for who farms, who makes and who bottles, and the product answers for vintage, tier and specification. The same premier cru made by different estates can differ greatly, so the site name alone is not enough.

Region and ingredients

Meursault lies on the Côte de Beaune of Burgundy in France, with slopes mainly facing east and south-east, gentle sun and a long ripening season. Soils are mostly limestone and marl and drain well, and differences in clay content between parcels directly shape body weight and the strength of minerality.

The village has no grand cru, and its highest tier is premier cru, an important grading difference from neighbouring Puligny-Montrachet. Meursault's premiers crus cluster on slopes to the south and north of the village, some parcels adjoining neighbouring communes, and the official delimitation governs the boundary.

The raw material is grape, with Chardonnay as the white variety. Water does not enter the finished wine, but rainfall and soil water-holding capacity affect ripening and dilution. Within one appellation, differences of slope position, soil depth and aspect are often more stable than vintage differences.

Winemaking

After harvest the grapes are pressed, the juice is settled and clarified, and yeast carries out fermentation. The traditional Meursault style often ferments or ages in oak, with the proportion of new barrels varying by estate and tier, bringing hazelnut, butter and toast while slow oxygen exposure adds texture.

Whether malolactic fermentation is allowed clearly changes acidity and creaminess. Some estates now use less new oak and stress fruit and minerality. These are producer style choices, and this entry gives no universal parameters; the estate's own information and the label govern.

Specification and label

The common volume is 750 mL, with alcohol by volume at about 12.5%–13.5% vol, varying with vintage, tier and estate. The label should be checked for village name, tier (village or premier cru), site name, vintage, bottler and importer.

A premier cru names its specific climat, while village wine usually says only Meursault. The Chinese back label, importer and anti-counterfeit information are the basis for checking identity; label languages differ by market and the bottle on hand governs, so label design alone cannot prove authenticity.

Flavour and tasting

Typical aromas are lemon, white flowers, hazelnut, butter and toasted bread, with honey and wet-stone minerality in some wines; the palate has medium-plus acidity, a medium to full body, a rounded texture and a finish of nuts and citrus peel. Village wine is usually more direct, while premiers crus show more structure and layering.

For tasting, serve at about 10–12 degrees Celsius in a Burgundy-shaped or larger white wine glass. Too cold suppresses hazelnut and toast, while too warm pushes alcohol forward. Watch whether oak covers the fruit, as excessive new barrels make the wine heavy and lose detail.

Drinking and pairing

It suits poultry, creamy sauces, lobster, shellfish and soft cheese; the wine's roundness and acidity carry butter and savoury depth. Avoid very hot or heavily vinegared dishes, which make the wine seem flat or sour.

Most village Meursault is for drinking within 3–8 years, while good premiers crus can last longer; the window depends on vintage and estate. After opening, refrigerate and finish within a few days. Excessive drinking harms health and minors should not drink.

Buying and storage

Buy from reputable channels and check village name, tier, site name, vintage and bottler information. Prices vary widely with tier, site and estate reputation, so no figure is given here. A premier cru is not automatically better, and how well style and vintage fit matters just as much.

For Meursault, unopened bottles should be kept away from light, lying flat in a cool place at an ideal 10–13 degrees Celsius, avoiding heat and sharp temperature swings. Cork-sealed bottles need humidity and can be stored lying down. After opening, refrigerate and drink soon rather than warming and cooling repeatedly.

Common misconceptions

First, treating Meursault as a brand. It is an appellation name. Second, assuming Meursault must have a grand cru. The highest tier in the village is premier cru. Third, equating premier cru with necessarily better. Site, vintage and estate style all shape the result. Fourth, believing white wine should have no oak. Moderate oak is part of the traditional Meursault style. Fifth, judging authenticity by price or label design. Channel, back label and bottler information are more reliable.
