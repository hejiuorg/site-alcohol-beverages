Chivas Regal 18 is an 18-year-old blended Scotch whisky from Chivas Regal, owned by Pernod Ricard and produced by Chivas Brothers. It is commonly bottled at 40% ABV. It is not a direct descendant of the 1909 "first luxury whisky"; it is the middle tier that Colin Scott added in 1997, above the 12-year-old and below the 25-year-old.

The 1997 Gap-Filler That Became an Icon

Chivas's own retirement notice for Colin Scott records the date precisely: Chivas 18 Gold Signature was launched in 1997 and repackaged in 2004, when Scott's gold signature was added to the bottle. Scott joined Chivas Brothers in 1973 and became the brand's fifth master blender in 1989. That timeline explains the product's job. It was not a six-year extension of the 12; it was a deliberate high-age expression placed between 12 and 25, at a price that could reach a wider audience than the 25-year-old.

Strathisla's 1786 Stone Walls

Chivas's home is Strathisla, which the company describes as established in 1786 and the oldest working distillery in the Scottish Highlands. The official line is unusually direct: Strathisla single malt "flows through every bottle of Chivas Regal". That is not decoration. It means the blend's backbone comes from a specific Highland distillery, not from an abstract idea of "Scottish flavour". It also explains why Chivas 18 leads with smoothness and orchard fruit rather than heavy smoke.

Broomhill Water and a 65.5–90 °C Mash

Chivas publishes a set of process figures that are worth keeping. Strathisla draws its water from the Broomhill Spring, about a mile away and exclusive to the distillery; the water is naturally filtered through granite. Mashing adds water in four stages, raising the overall temperature from 65.5 °C to 90 °C over about six hours. Fermentation takes roughly two days in traditional wooden washbacks. The distillery has four copper stills — two wash and two spirit — with low wines at about 25% ABV and a collected heart of about 65–70% ABV. The softness of Chivas 18 begins with those short-necked stills and that cut.

From Aberdeen to the 1909 Twenty-Five

Chivas's brand history reaches back to 1801, while the Chivas Brothers company history marks 1857, when John joined James and the brothers took over the Aberdeen shop. In 1843 the firm received its first Royal Warrant. In 1909, Charles Howard and Alexander Smith created a 25-year-old blend that the company calls the world's first luxury whisky. The 12-year-old followed in 1938. The 18 therefore sits in the middle of a very long sequence, not at its beginning.

What "Over 85 Flavour Notes" Actually Means

The official product page repeats a single number: over 85 flavour notes. The nose is dried fruit, buttery toffee and dark chocolate; the palate is velvety dark chocolate, floral notes and sweet, mellow smoke; the finish is warm and long. It is worth being precise about what that number is. "Over 85" is a count of tasting descriptors, not a laboratory measure, a score or a regulatory grade. It describes complexity, not quality. Chivas's own material also says the blend draws on more than twenty of Scotland's rare single malts.

Dark Chocolate, Dried Fruit, Cinnamon

Unpack the official vocabulary and a structure appears. Dried fruit and toffee supply sweetness and weight. Dark chocolate supplies a bitter frame. Cinnamon, orange peel and almond add spice and citrus edges. A little mellow smoke closes the finish. Compared with Ballantine's 17, the sweetness here is darker: dates, raisins, cocoa and cinnamon rather than honey and vanilla. That is why Chivas 18 works better with dark desserts and worse with delicate seafood.

The 12–18–25 Ladder

The Chivas age ladder can be read as 12 (launched in 1938, the everyday expression), 18 (launched in 1997, the premium middle) and 25 (the age of the original 1909 luxury blend, still represented at the top). The 18 occupies the "clearly aged but not yet flagship-priced" position. Its official and retail prices sit well above the 12 and below the 25. Judging value means reading that ladder together with the actual shelf price, not just the number on the label.

Forty Percent, Seventy-Five Centilitres

The official bottling data is 40% ABV and 75 cl. UK retail commonly uses 70 cl, which Tesco lists at about 28 alcohol units per bottle. Forty percent is the normal strength for blended Scotch; it does not mean the whisky has been "over-diluted", and it does not mean older expressions must be stronger. Both the 12 and the 18 can be 40%. Gift boxes are common, but the box does not change the age or the ABV inside; the bottle label remains the authority.

Why the UK Band Runs £54–£95

The UK market in November 2026 shows a wide spread. Tesco listed the 70 cl at £54.00, and price-comparison results for the standard bottle ran from £66.83 to £95.00. The £54 figure looks like a promotional price; £66–£95 is closer to the everyday band. Prices move with retailer, packaging, duty and promotion. The 18 sits above the 12 and below the 25, which is exactly where the ladder says it should be.

Pairing: Dark Chocolate and Roast Duck

Dried fruit, dark chocolate and cinnamon work with 70% dark chocolate, Christmas pudding, roast duck, blue cheese and orange-scented desserts. The bitterness meets fat, and the cinnamon picks up the spice in the duck. The same profile does not suit oysters or steamed sea bass: the oak and cocoa will bury the delicate sweetness of the fish. Ballantine's 17, with its honey and vanilla, is the better match for roast pork and aged Gouda. That is the division of labour between the two bottles.

The "Older Means Stronger" Myth

Age and alcohol strength are separate variables. Chivas 12 and Chivas 18 can both be 40% ABV; six extra years do not make the 18 "stronger". The second common error is treating 18 years as an average. The legal requirement is that every component in the bottle has matured for at least 18 years. The third is treating "over 85 flavour notes" as an official classification. It is tasting language, not an industry standard.

Buying and Keeping a Bottle

Check the 18-year statement, the 40% ABV and the importer information first, then decide whether the price is inside the normal band. After opening, store the bottle upright, away from light and heat, and keep the headspace small. If you drink only occasionally, a 700 mL bottle is more practical than a litre because the whisky stays fresher for longer. Unopened bottles do not need to be laid down, but they should not sit next to a hot stove or in direct sun.
