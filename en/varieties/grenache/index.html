Grenache

A heat- and drought-tolerant red grape with thin skins, fast sugar accumulation and low acidity, supporting fruit and alcohol in Rhône blends and in Spanish Priorat. DNA work confirms that Spain's Garnacha and Sardinia's Cannonau are the same variety.

Grenache is one of the most important red grapes of the Mediterranean: heat- and drought-tolerant, quick to accumulate sugar, able to survive on dry, poor slopes. Its skins are thin, its tannin medium and its acidity low, so on its own it can feel loose, yet in blends it supplies fruit, alcohol and spice. It is Garnacha in Spain and Cannonau in Sardinia, and genetic work confirms they are the same variety.

Figure: Grenache. Image: Jules Troncy, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Definition and synonyms

The English name is Grenache, the French Grenache Noir. It belongs to the European grape Vitis vinifera. Spain calls it Garnacha, Sardinia Cannonau, Catalonia Garnatxa, and the English-speaking world once used Alicante. These names can make labels look like different varieties when they refer to one plant. Colour variants include black, grey and white; Grenache Blanc and Grenache Gris can be made as single-varietal wines or used in blends.

Parentage and origin

The parents of Grenache are not fully established. Most research places its origin in Aragon in north-east Spain and has it spreading around the Mediterranean to southern France, Sardinia and southern Italy in the Middle Ages. Genetic analysis shows a relationship with old Spanish varieties but no confirmed parent pair. Its history in the Rhône goes back at least to the 18th century, and from the 19th century it became the mainstay of Southern Rhône blends.

Botanical character

Bunches are medium to large, berries medium with thin purple-red skins and juicy flesh. Thin skins mean colour and tannin are moderate, sugar rises quickly and acidity falls quickly, so alcohol can easily run high at ripeness. It buds early to medium, ripens mid-season, tolerates drought well and roots deeply, suiting bush vine (gobelet) training. Old-vine Grenache yields little and gives small berries and more concentrated flavour, though it is not automatically more disease-resistant.

Viticultural behaviour

Grenache tolerates heat, drought and wind and suits poor limestone, schist and gravel soils. It copes well with water stress, but in extreme drought it can stop ripening and shrivel. Thin skins make it sensitive to grey rot and sunburn, so picking avoids the hottest hours. Bush vines are common in the Southern Rhône and Spain, the canopy close to the ground for wind protection and soil warmth; mechanisation is spreading on flat sites.

French regions

The Southern Rhône is the core. Châteauneuf-du-Pape blends are Grenache-led, with the variety often 70% or more, supported by Syrah and Mourvèdre; Gigondas, Vacqueyras and Côtes du Rhône Villages also use it heavily. In Roussillon, Banyuls and Maury make vin doux naturel from Grenache, adding alcohol mid-fermentation to keep residual sugar. Languedoc uses it mostly for blends and rosé. The rounded stones and mistral of the Southern Rhône shape a Grenache that ripens without over-ripening.

Spain and Sardinia

Spain has the largest Grenache plantings. Campo de Borja and Calatayud in Aragon are known for old-vine Garnacha, while Priorat uses schist (llicorella) and steep old vines for concentrated, mineral wines and became a DOQ in 2009. Navarra and Catalonia also plant it. Cannonau is Sardinia's most important red grape, with a riper, spicier profile. McLaren Vale and Barossa in Australia hold large plantings of 1860s bush vines, an important resource for old-vine Grenache.

Winemaking and style

Winemaking varies widely. Reds often use whole clusters or partial carbonic maceration for aroma and softer tannin; rosé gets a short maceration for strawberry and citrus; vin doux naturel is fortified mid-fermentation to keep sugar and fruit. Low acidity and high pH make Grenache prone to oxidation and demand careful SO2 management. In blends, Syrah adds colour, tannin and black pepper, Mourvèdre structure, game and ageing potential, while Grenache adds fruit, alcohol and supple texture.

Sensory style

Young Grenache shows strawberry, raspberry, red cherry, white pepper and dried herbs (garrigue), with a medium to full body, soft tannin, low acidity and alcohol often between 14% and 15%. Old-vine versions are deeper in colour and more concentrated, with fig, leather and spice. Vin doux naturel brings blackberry, cocoa, coffee and caramel. Too warm a serving exaggerates alcohol, so a slight chill helps. It pairs well with roast meat, stews and Mediterranean spiced dishes.

The difference from Syrah and Mourvèdre

The three often appear together but play different roles. Grenache has thin skins, low tannin, low acid and high alcohol, supplying fruit and suppleness; Syrah has deep colour, medium tannin, higher acidity, black pepper and smoke; Mourvèdre has strong tannin, high acidity, deep colour, game and leather, and needs longer to ripen. Lumping them together misses the blend logic: Grenache provides the flesh, Syrah the frame, Mourvèdre the depth.

Producers and vintages

Southern Rhône names include Château Rayas, Château de Beaucastel and Domaine de la Janasse; Spain has Álvaro Palacios, Clos Mogador and Telmo Rodríguez; Australia has d'Arenberg and Yangarra. Vintage differences come mainly from drought and heat: dry years give low yields, small berries and concentration; hot years give more alcohol and less acidity. In the market, old-vine Garnacha has moved over the past two decades from cheap blending material to fine wine, with prices and attention rising clearly.

Common misconceptions

First, that Grenache is only for blends; single-varietal Garnacha and Cannonau are both common. Second, treating Grenache as a byword for high alcohol and low quality; old vines and altitude can bring balance. Third, that Grenache cannot age; good Châteauneuf-du-Pape and Priorat can keep for more than twenty years. Fourth, taking Garnacha and Cannonau for different varieties; DNA confirms they are the same. Fifth, assuming rosé is always sweet; Southern Rhône rosé is mostly dry. Sixth, thinking Grenache needs no acidity; low acid is its character, but a completely unbalanced wine feels loose.

For Grenache, begin with where it is grown. Then look at how it is labelled, and finally ask whether the style is dry or sweet. The answers point to different choices, not to a single score.

Wine explains variety character; France adds regional performance. Put Grenache between them before deciding what it is.
