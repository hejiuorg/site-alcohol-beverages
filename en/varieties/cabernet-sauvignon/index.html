Cabernet Sauvignon

A major red grape variety with small berries, thick skins and late ripening; it has high tannin and colour potential and originated in Bordeaux, now planted across the world's main wine regions.

Cabernet Sauvignon is a major red grape variety, botanically Vitis vinifera. Genetic research shows that its parents are Cabernet Franc and Sauvignon Blanc. This combination explains its blend of Cabernet Franc structure and Sauvignon Blanc aroma. The berries are small with thick, dark skins; the variety ripens late and has high tannin and pigment potential. It is one of the most widely planted red grape varieties in the world.

Figure: Cabernet Sauvignon. Photograph: Cabernet Sauvignon grapes from Château Cos D'Estournel. Credit: Megan Mallen, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source).

Definition and identity

Cabernet Sauvignon is a variety name, not a fixed flavour template. The same variety can be tight, high in tannin and marked by cedar and pencil lead on Bordeaux's left bank; riper and fuller in Napa Valley; and different again in Chile, Australia or Ningxia in China. A variety page describes botanical traits and winemaking potential; a wine page describes a particular product, and the two sit at different levels.

This distinction matters because the market contains both varietally labelled Cabernet and Cabernet-dominant blends. A single-variety wine emphasises the variety's expression, while a blend emphasises overall balance. The same name with different approaches requires checking the variety proportion and regional rules on the label rather than judging by variety name alone.

Botanical characteristics and parentage

Cabernet Sauvignon has relatively compact clusters and small berries with thick, blue-black skins. Thick skins mean high pigment and tannin potential and also make the variety more tolerant of transport and maceration than many others. It usually buds at a medium time and ripens late; in cool regions it may struggle to ripen fully and show green pepper or herbal notes, while in warm regions it more readily reaches blackcurrant and cedar ripeness.

The parentage explains its dual character: Cabernet Franc contributes structure, tannin and some herbal aromas, while Sauvignon Blanc contributes aromatic compounds and higher acidity. Cabernet's aroma therefore often carries blackcurrant, mint and green pepper, and these become more obvious when ripeness is insufficient. Understanding the parentage helps explain why the same variety varies so much between vintages.

Main regions

Cabernet Sauvignon originated in Bordeaux, France, and is the core variety of the left bank in Médoc and Graves. The gravel soils of Bordeaux's left bank drain well and warm up quickly, suiting late-ripening Cabernet; the right bank is Merlot-dominant, creating a clear contrast. Beyond Bordeaux it is planted on a commercial scale in Napa Valley in California, Chile, Australia, South Africa, Argentina, and Ningxia, Hebei and Xinjiang in China.

Differences in climate move Cabernet from tight, high-tannin styles towards riper, fuller-fruited styles. Wines from cool regions usually have higher acidity and more evident herbal notes; wines from warm regions have riper tannin and sweeter fruit. Soil, altitude and day-night temperature swings also affect the ripening rhythm, so experience from one region cannot simply be applied to another.

Climate and ripeness

Cabernet Sauvignon ripens late and needs enough heat accumulation to reach phenolic ripeness. When underripe, tannin may be coarse, aroma green, and green pepper and herbaceous notes pronounced; when overripe, acidity falls, fruit becomes jammy and the wine loses tension. Producers often pursue ripeness through yield control, improved airflow and delayed harvest, but each measure has a cost.

Harvest decisions also depend on the weather forecast and disease pressure. After rain, berries absorb water, diluting sugar and flavour, and rot risk rises. Many regions pick ahead of rain or harvest different parcels in separate passes. Vintage variation is pronounced in Cabernet, which is one reason the same wine differs so much from year to year.

Typical flavours and structure

Well-ripened Cabernet Sauvignon often shows blackcurrant, black cherry and plum aromas, with secondary notes of cedar, pencil lead, tobacco and mint. Its tannin is usually high, acidity medium to high and body full, making it suitable for long ageing. When young the tannin may feel tight; with bottle age it softens and the aroma moves from simple fruit towards more complex leather and woodland notes.

Green pepper, grass and herbal notes in underripe Cabernet are not a defect in themselves but a signal of ripeness and climate. Nor is a fully ripe blackcurrant-and-cedar combination proof of higher quality; the key question is whether tannin, acidity, fruit and body are balanced. Judging Cabernet requires looking at structure and aromatic complexity together.

Viticulture and winemaking

In the vineyard, Cabernet needs good drainage and enough heat; excessive nitrogen leads to over-vigorous canopy growth and uneven ripening. In winemaking, maceration time, punch-down frequency and fermentation temperature strongly affect tannin extraction; barrel size, the proportion of new wood and the toast level influence aroma and tannin texture.

Cabernet is also often blended with Merlot, Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot to adjust structure and aroma layers. Merlot can soften tannin and add roundness, Cabernet Franc adds aroma and acidity, and Petit Verdot contributes colour, tannin and spice. Single-variety Cabernet is also common in some New World regions, but blending remains the mainstream in Bordeaux.

Representative wines and relationships

In Bordeaux, Cabernet Sauvignon is the core of many classified growths; Château Lafite Rothschild is one of the left-bank representatives based mainly on Cabernet. Italy's "Super Tuscans" also use Cabernet, showing how the variety's expression changes with region and regulation. The United States, Chile and Australia have developed riper fruit styles, with some wines aged in a high proportion of new oak.

When judging Cabernet, variety is only the starting point; region, vintage, blend proportion and winemaking matter just as much. See red wine and wine for related styles. Treating the flavour of one famous wine as the variety standard ignores the legitimate expressions of other regions.

Common misconceptions

First, Cabernet Sauvignon is all Bordeaux red wine. Bordeaux's left bank is Cabernet-dominant, while the right bank is Merlot-dominant, and blend proportions vary widely.

Second, the stronger the tannin, the better. Tannin needs to balance acidity, fruit and body; excess makes the wine feel hard and dry.

Third, Cabernet must be aged before drinking. Many regional Cabernets have ample fruit when young, and the drinking window depends on the specific wine.

Fourth, green pepper is always a defect. Mild herbal notes may come from the variety and the vintage; only when they overwhelm the fruit is caution warranted.

Fifth, a variety label always means a high proportion. Variety labelling in blends is constrained by regional rules, so the specific proportion or rule should be checked.
