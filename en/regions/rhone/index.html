Rhône Valley

The Rhône Valley is a wine region along the Rhône in south-eastern France, running from Vienne south to Avignon. The north is a narrow band of steep slopes and the south is a broad valley, and the two differ markedly in climate, soils, varieties and rules while together forming France's second largest wine region band.

Figure: Rhône Valley. Image: Béa at French Wikipedia, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Geography and climate

The Rhône Valley runs north to south along the Rhône in a narrow band. The north is confined, with vineyards mostly on steep slopes and terraces; the south opens into a broad valley, with vineyards on alluvial plains, gravel terraces and foothills. The climate moves from continental in the north to Mediterranean in the south: northern winters are colder and the growing season shorter, while southern summers are hot, dry and sunny.

The mistral blows from north to south through the whole region, bringing dry air and lowering disease pressure, but also causing wind damage and cooling. Northern slopes need shelter and terraces, while the southern plain relies on gravel and rounded stones to store heat.

Northern Rhône

Northern Rhône vineyards sit on steep slopes on both banks of the Rhône, with granite, schist and gneiss soils that drain well and are shallow, often requiring terraces and manual work. Reds are made from Syrah as the only permitted variety; Côte-Rôtie allows a small addition of Viognier in the fermentation. Whites are mainly Viognier, Marsanne and Roussanne.

Main appellations include Côte-Rôtie, Condrieu, Saint-Joseph, Crozes-Hermitage, Hermitage, Cornas and Saint-Péray. Outputs are small, and the style is known for black pepper, violet, olive and smoke, usually firmer in structure than the south.

Southern Rhône

The southern valley is wider, with vineyards on the plains north of Avignon, on gravel terraces and at the foot of the Dentelles de Montmirail. Soils are mainly alluvial gravel, clay-limestone and marl, and some plots are covered with the famous rounded stones (galets roulés) that store heat by day and release it at night. Reds are based on Grenache, often blended with Syrah, Mourvèdre and Cinsault.

Main appellations include Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Gigondas, Vacqueyras, Tavel, Lirac and the regional Côtes du Rhône. The style is known for ripe black fruit, Provençal herbs and spice, with a fuller body. White and rosé wines also account for a share of output.

Varieties and styles

Varieties and styles divide by north and south: the north centres on Syrah, with reds as varietal wines or with a small addition of Viognier, firmer in style with clear pepper and floral notes; whites are mainly Viognier, Marsanne and Roussanne. The south centres on Grenache, often blended with Syrah, Mourvèdre and others, with a fuller body and riper fruit.

Red wine dominates both parts. The choice of fermentation and oak ageing shapes the style: whole-cluster fermentation adds spice and herbal layers, while the share of new oak affects vanilla, smoke and texture.

Classification and rules

The Rhône Valley belongs to the French AOC/AOP system, administered by INAO within the EU framework, covering the area, permitted varieties, variety proportions, yield limits, pruning, harvesting, minimum alcohol and labelling. Northern rules centre on Syrah as a single variety, while southern rules centre on Grenache and list the permitted blending varieties.

The hierarchy runs from regional Côtes du Rhône through village level to specific appellations; separate appellations such as Châteauneuf-du-Pape have their own rules. When reading a label, separate the regional, village and specific appellation names, which stand for different areas and levels of strictness.

Representative wines and producers

Representative northern appellations include Côte-Rôtie, Cornas, Saint-Joseph and Crozes-Hermitage; representative southern appellations include Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Gigondas and Vacqueyras.

The region has large houses, cooperatives and family estates. Bottler information on the label is the key to telling origin, and estates and vintages within one appellation differ markedly, so one wine cannot stand for the whole area.

Market and drinking

Rhône reds usually come in 750 mL bottles, with alcohol by volume of about 12.5%–14% vol in the north and about 13%–15.5% vol in the south. Northern reds suit red meat, grilled meat and game; southern reds suit stews, grilled meat and hard cheese.

Serve at about 16–18 °C, and decant firmly structured wines. Most wines suit drinking within a few years and top wines can go longer; the window varies by appellation, estate and vintage. Excessive drinking is harmful to health, and minors should not drink.

Common misconceptions

First, treating the Rhône Valley as a single style. North and south differ greatly in varieties, climate and rules. Second, believing the northern Rhône is also Grenache-led. Northern reds are made from Syrah as the only permitted variety. Third, believing the southern Rhône is only Grenache. Blends also include Syrah, Mourvèdre and Cinsault. Fourth, treating the mistral only as a hazard. It also brings dry air and lowers disease pressure. Fifth, confusing regional Côtes du Rhône with separate appellations such as Châteauneuf-du-Pape.

Scope note

This page describes the framework, varieties and rules of the northern and southern Rhône and does not assess individual estates or vintages. The parent region is France; specific wine pages record producers and products, separate from the region level covered here.

Short definition: A wine region along the Rhône in south-eastern France, running from Vienne to Avignon; the north is a narrow band of steep slopes where reds are made from Syrah alone, the south is a broad valley where reds are Grenache-led blends, and the mistral blows through the whole region.

Start with the appellation and its permitted varieties. From there, consider the vintage and the producer, then how the wine is labelled. That is how a practical judgment about Rhône Valley is built.

Start with France for geographic boundary, then read Syrah for climate and soil. Seeing both keeps Rhône Valley from being confused with a neighbouring idea.
