Provence

A wine region on the Mediterranean coast of south-east France, running from the Rhône delta to west of Nice; best known for rosé, it also makes red and white wines, mainly from Grenache, Cinsault, Syrah and Mourvèdre, with Bandol and other appellations under their own rules.

Provence is the wine region most easily defined by the word "rosé", and also one of the most obscured by it. Its coast runs from the Rhône delta to west of Nice, with vineyards on limestone hills, schist slopes and coastal plains. Rosé is indeed dominant, but the Mourvèdre reds of Bandol, the whites of Cassis and the rare blends of Palette are the way into the region's depth. Provence is not a single style but a group of AOCs that share a Mediterranean climate while keeping separate rules and histories.

Figure: Provence. Image: Robert Brink, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Geography: from the Rhône delta to the Côte d'Azur

Provence spans parts of Var, Bouches-du-Rhône, Alpes-Maritimes and Alpes-de-Haute-Provence. The western end approaches the Rhône Valley and the eastern end reaches the hinterland of Nice; the coast includes Marseille, Toulon, Cannes and Nice, while inland cities include Aix-en-Provence and Draguignan. Vineyards mostly sit on coastal hills and inland highlands, from sea level to about 500 metres.

Climate: Mediterranean and the mistral

The region has a classic Mediterranean climate: hot dry summers, mild winters, abundant sunshine and low annual rainfall. The mistral blows south from the Rhône Valley, reducing humidity and disease pressure and thickening grape skins and concentrating aromas. Drought is the main limit and irrigation is regulated; heatwaves and harvest rain can sharply change a vintage.

Soils and terrain

Provence has very diverse soils: limestone and gravel near the coast, fast-draining with deep roots; schist, sandstone and clay on inland highlands, with better water retention. Bandol's slopes are mainly limestone and gravel, while Cassis has limestone cliffs and terraces. These differences explain why two rosés from different villages can differ so much in colour, acidity and aroma.

Legal framework: several AOCs, separate rules

The main appellations are Côtes de Provence, Coteaux d'Aix-en-Provence, Coteaux Varois en Provence, Bandol, Cassis, Palette, Bellet and Pierrevert. Côtes de Provence is the largest, covering much of Var and Bouches-du-Rhône; Bandol obtained AOC status in 1941, Cassis in 1936 and Palette in 1948.

The four supplementary names of Côtes de Provence

Since 2019 Côtes de Provence has allowed four supplementary geographical names on labels: Sainte-Victoire, Fréjus, La Londe and Pierrefeu. They correspond to different soils, altitudes and sea influences: Sainte-Victoire lies closer to the inland mountains, while La Londe and Pierrefeu are more moderated by the sea. Seeing one of these names means the wine comes from a more specific parcel rather than the whole region.

Varieties: Grenache, Cinsault, Syrah and Mourvèdre

Rosé and reds rely on Grenache, Cinsault, Syrah and Mourvèdre, with Carignan, Tibouren and other local varieties in support. Mourvèdre leads in Bandol, giving structure, spice and ageing potential; Grenache adds red fruit and alcohol; Cinsault brings lightness and flowers; Syrah contributes pepper and colour. Whites use Rolle, also called Vermentino, Clairette, Ugni Blanc, Marsanne and Sauvignon.

Bandol: minimum Mourvèdre and long ageing

Bandol is the most structured red appellation in Provence. The rules require reds to contain at least 50% Mourvèdre, blended with Grenache, Cinsault and others, and to age in oak for at least 18 months. The appellation covers about 1550 hectares, with a base yield cap of 35 hl/ha. The wines show blackberry, black olive, leather, garrigue and sea air, with firm tannin and good medium-term ageing.

Cassis, Palette and Bellet: small and different whites

Cassis is known for white wine, with Marsanne, Clairette, Ugni Blanc and Sauvignon, showing citrus, white flowers, fennel and sea salt, and suits seafood. Palette is tiny and makes red, white and rosé, blending Clairette, Grenache and Mourvèdre among others, with a history reaching back to antiquity. Bellet sits in the hills behind Nice and is known for Rolle and Chardonnay whites and Braquet reds, in very small volumes.

Producers

Leading Côtes de Provence names include Domaines Ott, Château d'Esclans and Whispering Angel, Château de Berne and Domaines Bunan; Bandol has Domaine Tempier, Château Pradeaux, Domaine du Gros'Noré and Château de la Bégude; Cassis has Clos Sainte-Magdeleine and Domaine du Bagnol. They represent three routes: commercial rosé, traditional Bandol and small specialist appellations.

Vintages and market

Provence AOC rosé is about 85% of regional production. In 2024 AOP rosé output was about 134 million bottles, and Provence accounts for 40%-45% of French rosé production. The market runs from everyday rosé at 8-20 euros, to leading estates at 20-40 euros, to Bandol reds above 30 euros. Vintage variation comes mainly from drought, heatwaves and harvest rain; rosé is usually best within 1-3 years, while Bandol red can age for 10 years or more.

Drinking and pairing

Serve rosé at 8-10℃ with seafood, salads, Provençal stews and Mediterranean starters; Bandol red at 16-18℃ with grilled meat, game and hard cheese; Cassis white with oysters, sea fish and fennel dishes. Once opened, refrigerate and drink soon; excessive drinking is harmful to health, and minors should not drink.

Common misconceptions

First, thinking Provence makes only rosé; it also makes structured reds and fine whites. Second, thinking paler rosé is better; colour mainly reflects skin contact and variety. Third, conflating Provence with the Rhône Valley; they are neighbours but have different AOCs and variety proportions. Fourth, thinking rosé cannot age; a few structured examples can be kept short term, but most are best early. Fifth, reading only "Côtes de Provence" and ignoring the supplementary name; a village often says more about style.

In brief: A wine region on the Mediterranean coast of south-east France, running from the Rhône delta to west of Nice; best known for rosé, it also makes red and white wines, mainly from Grenache, Cinsault, Syrah and Mourvèdre, with Bandol and other appellations under their own rules.

For Provence, begin with the appellation and its permitted varieties, then the vintage and the producer, and finish with how the wine is labelled; that order keeps the account grounded.
