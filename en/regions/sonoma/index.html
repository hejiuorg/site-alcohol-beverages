Sonoma is the wine region of Sonoma County, California, known for varied microclimates and a dense set of AVAs, running from the cool Pacific coast to warm inland valleys.

Figure: Sonoma. Image: Cullen328, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Definition and scope

Sonoma is a wine region inside Sonoma County, California. The county covers more than one million acres, of which about 60,000 acres (about 24,300 hectares) are planted to vines, roughly 6% of the county. The county has 19 federally approved AVAs and more than 1,800 grape growers.

Compared with Napa Valley, Sonoma is larger, has more AVAs and a wider climate range. The county contains both cool marine-influenced zones and warm inland valleys, so Pinot Noir, Chardonnay, Cabernet Sauvignon and Zinfandel can all be produced within one county.

History

Sonoma is one of California's earliest grape-growing areas, with European grapes (Vitis vinifera) recorded there in 1817. In the mid-nineteenth century missionaries and settlers established vineyards and wineries, and Sonoma became an early centre of California wine.

The industry shrank through vine disease and Prohibition in the early twentieth century, then expanded again after the 1970s. In the 1980s and 1990s cool AVAs such as Russian River Valley and Sonoma Coast were established, and the reputation of Pinot Noir and Chardonnay rose. In 2022 West Sonoma Coast became a new AVA.

Geography and climate

Sonoma County faces the Pacific on the west and inland mountains on the east, with terrain running from coast and mountains to valleys and terraces. The cold Pacific current and sea fog push into the river valleys, making the west and Russian River Valley cool during the growing season; inland areas such as Alexander Valley, Knights Valley and Sonoma Valley are warmer, reaching about 21–32 degrees Celsius by day in summer and falling to about 4–10 at night.

Little rain falls from May to September, so the growing season is long and dry, with winter rain recharging soil water. Elevation runs from sea level to about 2,600 feet (800 metres), and slope and valley floor differ clearly in temperature, drainage and ripening pace.

Geological history has produced 11 major geological formations and 31 soil series, including volcanic rock, marine sediment, alluvial fans and river terraces. Well-drained, shallow hillside soils suit Pinot Noir and Zinfandel; deeper, water-retentive valley soils suit Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot. These differences form the basis of terroir.

AVAs and sub-regions

Sonoma's 19 AVAs cover coast, mountains, valleys and terraces, and include:

  • Russian River Valley: influenced by sea fog and cool air, known for Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.
  • Sonoma Coast and West Sonoma Coast: close to the Pacific, cool and windy, mainly Pinot Noir and Chardonnay; West Sonoma Coast was approved in 2022 and has about 1,028 acres of vineyard.
  • Alexander Valley: warm inland, mainly Cabernet Sauvignon, with Merlot and Zinfandel too.
  • Dry Creek Valley: known for Zinfandel, with Syrah and Petite Sirah as well.
  • Sonoma Valley and Knights Valley: inland valleys growing Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Zinfandel.
  • Other AVAs include Chalk Hill, Bennett Valley, Green Valley of Russian River Valley, Petaluma Gap, Sonoma Mountain, Rockpile, Fort Ross–Seaview, Moon Mountain District, Fountaingrove District and Carneros (shared with Napa).

An AVA only sets a geographic boundary and does not prescribe varieties or styles. Russian River Valley also has warm sites planted to Cabernet, and Alexander Valley has cool sites, so an AVA name on a label should be read together with the specific site.

Varieties and raw material

Sonoma's variety structure is more dispersed than Napa Valley's. Chardonnay and Pinot Noir dominate the cool west, Cabernet Sauvignon stands out in inland areas such as Alexander Valley and Sonoma Valley, and Zinfandel has a long history in Dry Creek Valley and Sonoma Valley. Other varieties include Merlot, Syrah, Petite Sirah, Sauvignon Blanc and Viognier.

The raw material is grape. About 99% of Sonoma County vineyard is sustainably certified, covering water use, pest management and ecological protection. Variety choice is closely tied to the AVA climate gradient: cool zones suit early-ripening varieties that hold acidity, while warm inland zones suit late-ripening varieties that need heat.

Winemaking

Red grapes are macerated and aged according to variety and AVA. Pinot Noir usually sees shorter maceration, cooler fermentation and older barrels to keep fruit and acidity; Cabernet Sauvignon and Zinfandel see longer maceration and often age in a mix of new and used oak. Zinfandel ripens unevenly, so some wineries pick in passes or field-sort.

Whites ferment cool. Some Chardonnay sees oak, lees stirring and malolactic fermentation, while Sauvignon Blanc is mostly kept in stainless steel for freshness. Sparkling wine is also made in Russian River Valley and Sonoma Coast, usually by the traditional method.

AVA and labelling

For Sonoma, US appellations of origin are governed by 27 CFR 4.25. A wine labelled with an AVA must derive at least 85% of its volume from grapes grown inside that AVA; for a county or state appellation the figure falls to 75%. The wine must also be fully finished in the state where the labelled area lies, with limited exceptions.

An AVA is a geographic name, not a quality grade; differences between wineries, vintages and sites are far greater than the AVA name itself. Sonoma has many AVAs, and a more specific place name on a label usually means a more concentrated source, but not necessarily higher quality.

Labels and serving

The label should be checked for the AVA name, variety, vintage, alcohol, volume and bottler. A wine labelled as a single variety must meet the federal variety share; blends must list their varieties under the rules. Pinot Noir is served at about 14–16 degrees Celsius, Chardonnay at about 10–12, Cabernet Sauvignon and Zinfandel at about 16–18 and Sauvignon Blanc at about 8–10.

Pinot Noir suits poultry, mushrooms and light red meat; Chardonnay suits seafood and creamy dishes; Cabernet Sauvignon and Zinfandel suit grilled meat, red meat and tomato-based dishes. Refrigerate after opening and finish soon. Excessive drinking harms health and minors should not drink.

Common misconceptions

First, treating Sonoma as "another Napa". Sonoma is larger, has more AVAs, a wider climate range and a more dispersed variety structure. Second, thinking Sonoma grows only Pinot Noir. Pinot Noir is concentrated in the cool west, and Cabernet Sauvignon, Zinfandel and Chardonnay matter just as much. Third, treating an AVA as a quality grade. An AVA only defines a geographic area and does not judge wine quality. Fourth, assuming a coastal AVA is always cooler. Sea fog and terrain act together, and gaps such as Petaluma Gap are cooler and windier than other sites on the same coast. Fifth, treating West Sonoma Coast and Sonoma Coast as the same AVA. The former is a separate AVA approved in 2022, with a boundary further west.

For Sonoma, begin with the appellation and its permitted varieties. Then look at the vintage and the producer, and finally ask how the wine is labelled. The answers point to different choices, not to a single score.
