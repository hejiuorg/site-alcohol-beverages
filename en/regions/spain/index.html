Spain

A major wine-producing country in southern Europe, known for Tempranillo, Garnacha and its DO hierarchy; Rioja, Ribera del Duero, Cava and Jerez make up a varied drinks landscape.

Spain is a major wine-producing country in southern Europe, with extensive vineyards and many levels of appellation. Its drinks landscape includes Rioja reds centred on Tempranillo, Cava sparkling wine, Jerez fortified wines and white wines from Galicia. Understanding Spain means looking at varieties, regions, classification and ageing traditions together.

Figure: Spain. Photograph: Uga - Lanzarote - Spain. Vineyards -U16. Credit: Luis Miguel Bugallo Sánchez, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source).

Geography and climate

Spain occupies most of the Iberian Peninsula. Much of the country has a Mediterranean or continental climate, with hot, dry summers and mild or cold winters. The north and north-west are influenced by the Atlantic, with more rainfall and a wetter climate; the central plateau is high and has a wide diurnal range; Andalusia in the south is hot and dry; Catalonia in the north-east faces the Mediterranean. Altitude, sea currents and mountains create many microclimates within one country.

Water is a key limit in many regions. Dry areas use variety choice, planting density and irrigation management to maintain ripeness and acidity; the wetter north-west must deal with disease pressure and under-ripening. Climate is not a backdrop but the main variable affecting yield and style each year.

History

Spanish viticulture and winemaking go back to antiquity, and monasteries and local towns preserved the early outlines of appellations through the Middle Ages. In the late nineteenth century, French vineyards were hit by disease and some merchants and technicians turned to Spain, advancing winemaking and trade. The DO system was built up through the twentieth century and later aligned with the EU PDO framework, making regional rules more consistent.

Wine regions

  • Rioja: mainly Tempranillo, often blended with Garnacha and Graciano, with a deep oak-ageing tradition.
  • Ribera del Duero: continental climate, with more concentrated, structured Tempranillo.
  • Catalonia: Cava sparkling wine and Priorat reds.
  • Galicia: white wines under a strong Atlantic influence.
  • Andalusia: Jerez fortified wines and the sherry system.
  • Castilla-La Mancha: large volumes, high heat, with irrigation and variety choice shaping style.

Varieties and styles

Tempranillo is Spain's signature red variety, with relatively thick skins, early ripening and notes of cherry, leather and tobacco. Rioja Tempranillo often undergoes oak ageing for a rounder structure, while Ribera del Duero is tighter. Garnacha is widely planted in the north-east and south, bringing red fruit and often higher alcohol.

Beyond red wine, Spain also produces white wine, sparkling wine and fortified wine. Cava is based mainly on local white varieties and uses the traditional method for a second fermentation; sherry in Jerez is fortified after fermentation and ranges from bone dry to sweet. Different categories are judged by different standards and cannot be compared using red-wine criteria alone.

Representative wines and types

Traditional wines from Rioja and Ribera del Duero are usually based on Tempranillo and, after different periods of barrel ageing, form a spectrum from young fruit to mature spice. Cava is Spain's representative sparkling wine, suited to before-meal and celebration occasions; Jerez produces fortified wines from very dry to sweet. Spain also produces brandy, with Jerez-area brandy having its own regional and ageing rules.

A specific wine also depends on the producer, vintage, blend proportions and ageing. The region provides the framework; the wine page records the individual product.

Regulation and classification

Spanish wine appellations are built around DO (Denominación de Origen), linked to the EU PDO system; some leading regions have higher tiers such as DO Pago. Cava and Jerez have their own regional and labelling rules. Classification sets varieties, yields, ageing and label terms, but is not a quality ranking.

When reading a label, distinguish the country name, DO name, estate name and type name. Quality can vary greatly between estates within one DO, and ageing terms have different meanings depending on regional rules.

Market and drinking

Spain is a major wine producer and exporter, with domestic consumption centred on restaurants and everyday drinking. Wine spans a wide price range, from everyday table wine to long-aged fine wines. Reds suit meat, ham and cheese; whites suit seafood and lighter food; sparkling and fortified wines have their own occasions.

Serving temperature and storage after opening affect the experience. For a specific wine, the DO, vintage, alcohol content and type on the label are the most direct information.

Common misconceptions

First, that Spanish wine is always sweet or strong. It includes dry reds and whites, sparkling and fortified wines.

Second, that Rioja is all of Spain. Spain also has Ribera del Duero, Priorat, Jerez and many other regions.

Third, that Tempranillo is Spain's Cabernet Sauvignon. The two differ in aroma, acidity and tannin structure.

Fourth, that a higher DO tier is always better. Classification guarantees rules, not sensory quality in every bottle.

Fifth, that sherry is always sweet. Sherry ranges from bone dry to sweet, a very wide span.
