Yamanashi

Yamanashi lies in central Honshu, west of Tokyo, and is the most concentrated wine region in Japan. Centred on the Kofu basin and Katsunuma in Koshu city, it makes white wine from the local flagship variety Koshu, along with a smaller amount of red wine, and holds a special place in Japanese domestic wine.

Geography and climate

The core of the region is the Kofu basin. Mountains — the Chichibu range, the Southern Alps and the peaks toward Mount Fuji — surround the basin, and rivers such as the Fuefuki and the Hikawa flow out of the mountains to build alluvial fans. Vineyards sit mostly on the better-drained middle and upper parts of these fans. Soils are mainly gravelly alluvium with volcanic debris, draining quickly and warming early.

The climate is a humid temperate monsoon type, with rainy, humid summers; the rainy season and typhoon rainfall are the main source of disease pressure. Compared with dry European regions, the key work in Yamanashi is not water control but disease control: training systems, canopy management and the picking window are all organised around rain. Day-night temperature swings and cool air draining from the mountains at night help retain acidity.

The basin mostly sits at 300–600 metres, with steep mountain walls above the vineyards. This "small basin at the foot of the mountains" topography means plots are generally small and mechanisation is limited.

History

Grape growing around Katsunuma goes back to the Edo period, and commercial winemaking was established there by the early Meiji era. The Dai-Nippon Yamanashi Wine Company founded at Katsunuma in 1877 is often treated as one of the starting points of the modern Japanese wine industry, and its business continued into today's Mercian.

In the later twentieth century, demand for Japanese domestic wine and grape-picking tourism grew together, and Yamanashi became the main supplier of domestic wine. In recent years the number of wineries has increased, and styles have moved from the sweeter, fruitier domestic wines of the early period toward dry wines that emphasise variety and site.

Varieties and styles

Koshu is the signature variety of the region. Its skin is pinkish-grey, and research indicates that it carries ancestry from an East Asian wild grape, unlike pure European wine grapes. It tolerates humidity and suits Japan's rainy climate, ripens relatively early, and is one of the few wine grapes that ripens reliably in Yamanashi.

White wine from Koshu is usually light to medium in body, with clear acidity and restrained aromas of citrus, white peach, pear and a little white flower; it is not an aromatic style. Winemaking often uses cool fermentation to preserve aroma, and some wines spend time on the lees for texture; oak is used cautiously, since too much barrel can bury the variety's delicacy.

Red wine is a small share, mostly Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon and local hybrids, in a medium-bodied, softly tannic style. Koshu can also be made into orange wine and sparkling wine, but both remain small in volume and market presence.

Sub-zones

  • Katsunuma: the Katsunuma area of Koshu city, the oldest and most densely planted part of the region and the core of Koshu.
  • Fuefuki: around Fuefuki city, continuous with Katsunuma, with vineyards spread across the alluvial fans.
  • Kai and Nirasaki: north and west of the basin, with fewer wineries and more scattered plots.
  • Otsuki and the Fuji area: higher, cooler ground with a later ripening season.

There is no legal hierarchy among these sub-zones; the differences come mainly from altitude, position on the fans and rainfall distribution.

Regulation and labelling

Origin labelling for Japanese wine falls under the Geographical Indication (GI) protection system administered by the National Tax Agency, and registered names must meet defined origin and production requirements. "Yamanashi" has been entered as a wine GI under this system; the exact area and variety requirements follow the authority's register.

Japanese domestic wine is also governed by the liquor tax law and food labelling rules. "Japanese wine" and "domestic wine" are not fully equivalent in the market: the latter can mean wine made in Japan from grapes or juice that are partly imported, so the label's statement of grape origin is what to read.

Representative wines and producers

  • Grace Wine (Chuo Budoshu): a long-established Katsunuma winery known for Koshu and Koshu-based whites, and one of the faces of Yamanashi's international style.
  • Marufuji Wine: one of the older Katsunuma wineries, with a range covering Koshu whites and reds.
  • Mercian: descended from the 1877 Dai-Nippon Yamanashi Wine Company, a major Japanese wine company with production and vineyards in Yamanashi.
  • Tomi no Oka (Suntory): another large Yamanashi winery, known for its own vineyards and visitor facilities.

Common misconceptions

First, treating Koshu as either a pure Japanese native or a European variety. It is a local variety with East Asian wild-grape ancestry, sitting between the two. Second, assuming all Yamanashi grapes are wine grapes. Table grapes such as Kyoho take up a large share of the area, in a separate system from wine grapes. Third, thinking all Japanese domestic wine is imported bulk wine. Yamanashi does have products from imported material, but also wineries working with local grapes, and labels distinguish them. Fourth, believing a rainy region cannot make high-acid white wine. Koshu's high acidity is exactly the result of the local climate and early picking. Fifth, treating Katsunuma as a legal appellation tier. It is first of all a historic and geographical core.

In brief: Japan's core wine region, centred on the Kofu basin and Katsunuma; Koshu is the signature local white variety, and alluvial-fan gravel soils amid the mountains around Mount Fuji give taut, restrained whites with clear acidity, while Katsunuma is also one of the birthplaces of the modern Japanese wine industry.

Yamanashi is best approached through the appellation and its permitted varieties, then the vintage and the producer, and then how the wine is labelled. One check alone will not settle it.
