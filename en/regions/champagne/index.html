Champagne

A sparkling wine appellation in northern France, centred on Reims and Épernay, with vineyards in the Montagne de Reims, the Vallée de la Marne, the Côte des Blancs and the Côte des Bar to the south. It is made from Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier by the traditional method of in-bottle second fermentation, aged on the lees and labelled by sweetness level.

Champagne is the sparkling wine appellation of the Champagne region in northern France and the only legal home of the protected name Champagne. Vineyards surround Reims and Épernay and extend south to the Côte des Bar. The main varieties are Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier.

Figure: Champagne. Image: bgvjpe, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Geography and climate

Champagne lies in northern France, with vineyards in parts of the Marne, Aisne, Aube and Haute-Marne departments. The climate is cool with an oceanic influence and a short growing season; spring frost, summer hail and autumn rain are the main risks. Low temperatures let the grapes keep high acidity, which is the backbone of the Champagne style.

Chalk is the most representative soil, especially in the Montagne de Reims and the Côte des Blancs: it drains well, reflects sunlight and stores heat, encouraging deep roots and ripening. The Vallée de la Marne has more varied soils with a higher share of clay and sand, which suit Pinot Meunier.

Sub-regions

The vineyards are usually grouped into four main areas:

  • Montagne de Reims: mainly Pinot Noir, with fuller fruit and structure.
  • Vallée de la Marne: a higher share of Pinot Meunier, with forward fruit and earlier ripening.
  • Côte des Blancs: mainly Chardonnay, with chalk slopes giving high acidity and minerality.
  • Côte des Bar: in the south of the region, mainly Pinot Noir, historically part of the Aube.

Villages in the appellation are classed as grand cru or premier cru, with 17 grand cru villages and 42 premier cru villages at present. Village classification reflects historical prices and site reputation, not a quality grade for a wine; estates within one village still differ markedly.

Varieties and styles

Chardonnay brings acidity, citrus and white flowers, and later cream and nut notes with age; Pinot Noir brings body, red fruit and structure; Pinot Meunier brings fruit and suppleness and ripens earlier, which matters in the frost-prone Vallée de la Marne.

Most Champagne is a blend of base wines from different varieties, villages and years, balanced for acidity, fruit and structure. Single-variety, single-village and single-year wines also exist but are a minority, and the exact proportions belong to the estate.

Winemaking

Champagne uses the traditional method. Grapes are harvested by hand in whole bunches as the rules require, pressed, and given a first fermentation to make base wine; some estates also run malolactic fermentation to soften acidity. The base wines are then blended, bottled with sugar and yeast, and undergo a second fermentation in bottle that creates the bubbles.

After the second fermentation the wine ages on the lees. Non-vintage Champagne ages in the cellar for at least 15 months, of which at least 12 months are on the lees; vintage Champagne ages for at least 36 months (3 years), again with at least 12 months on the lees. Longer ageing gives finer bubbles and more brioche and nut notes.

At the end of ageing the bottles are riddled to gather the lees at the neck and disgorged; a dosage is then added to reach the target sweetness. This sequence is what separates Champagne from tank-fermented sparkling wines.

Classification and rules

Champagne belongs to the French AOC/AOP system, administered by INAO within the EU framework, covering the area, permitted varieties, yield limits, pruning, harvesting, pressing ratios, second fermentation, minimum ageing and labelling. Only sparkling wine that meets the rules may use the name Champagne.

The Champagne area also contains separate appellations such as Coteaux Champenois for still wines and Rosé des Riceys for still pink wine; they share the geography but do not use the Champagne name. When reading a label, separate the appellation, village classification, estate name and sweetness designation.

Sweetness labelling

Sweetness in Champagne is set by the dosage added after disgorgement, and the common label grades are defined by residual sugar content:

  • Brut Nature: below 3 g/L.
  • Extra Brut: 0–6 g/L.
  • Brut: below 12 g/L.
  • Extra Dry (Extra Sec): 12–17 g/L.
  • Sec: 17–32 g/L.
  • Demi-Sec: 32–50 g/L.
  • Doux: above 50 g/L.

A sweetness designation describes sugar content, not a quality grade. Most wines are Brut, while Demi-Sec and Doux suit dessert; the figures are an upper framework and the actual content is estate information.

Vintage and non-vintage

Non-vintage Champagne is a blend of base wines from several years, aiming at a consistent estate style, and makes up most of the market. The blend proportions belong to the estate, and the label usually names only the house and the sweetness level.

Vintage Champagne is made only from grapes harvested in the stated year and requires longer lees ageing. Vintage wines are produced only in better years and in smaller quantity; the year directly affects acidity, ripeness and ageing potential.

Representative wines and producers

Champagne has three kinds of producer: large houses, cooperatives and independent estates. Moët, Veuve Clicquot and Krug sit at the large-house level, and Dom Pérignon is a flagship vintage wine from a house. Cooperatives pool the fruit of many growers, while independent estates mostly own their plots and bottle their own wine.

The bottler abbreviation on the label distinguishes producer types: NM for a négociant house, RM for a grower who bottles their own wine, and CM for a cooperative. Together with the appellation, vintage and sweetness designation, it forms the basic information for judging a bottle's origin.

Market and drinking

The usual bottle is 750 mL, with alcohol by volume typically about 12%–12.5% vol. Champagne suits the start of a meal and pairs with seafood, white meat and salty snacks; sweeter wines suit fruit desserts.

Serve at about 8–10 °C in a tulip or flute glass. Most non-vintage wines suit drinking within 2–5 years and vintage wines can go longer; bubbles fade after opening, so refrigerate and drink soon. Excessive drinking is harmful to health, and minors should not drink.

Common misconceptions

First, calling every sparkling wine Champagne. Champagne is a protected French appellation name. Second, thinking sweeter Champagne is better. The sweetness designation describes sugar content, and matching the style matters more. Third, treating bubble size as the only quality measure. Aroma, acidity and balance matter equally. Fourth, believing only Chardonnay can make Champagne. Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier are major varieties too. Fifth, treating non-vintage Champagne as low quality. Non-vintage is a blending approach for consistency and is not tied to quality. Sixth, treating village classification as a wine rating. Grand cru and premier cru describe historical site reputation, not the level of every bottle.

Advanced judgment

Figure: Vineyard at Trépail on the Montagne de Reims, a typical Champagne landscape of chalk soil and slope aspect. Image: Havang(nl), CC0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Sub-region Main grapes Style clue
Montagne de Reims Pinot Noir dominant Structure, red fruit, ageing potential
Côte des Blancs Chardonnay Acidity, florals, minerality
Vallée de la Marne Meunier dominant Fruit, suppleness, earlier drinking
Southern vineyards Chardonnay dominant Lightness, freshness, value

Is Blanc de Blancs always from the Côte des Blancs? No. Blanc de Blancs means white grapes only, usually Chardonnay in Champagne, but the fruit can come from several sub-regions. The Côte des Blancs is a common source, not the only one.

Is non-vintage Champagne always inferior to vintage? No. Non-vintage blends several years for consistency; vintage expresses one year. They serve different aims, not a quality hierarchy.

How should I choose a Champagne sweetness level? Start with occasion and food: Brut suits most savoury dishes, Demi-Sec suits desserts and mild heat. Do not reduce “Brut” to “dry”; residual sugar varies between houses.

Scope note

This region describes the area, sub-regions, varieties, process and labelling rules of Champagne and does not assess individual estates or vintages. The parent region is France; Champagne wine pages record specific producers and products, separate from the region level covered here.
