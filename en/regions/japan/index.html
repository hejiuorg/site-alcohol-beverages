Japan

An island country in East Asia best known for sake; shochu, whisky and beer are also established, and sake's specific-name categories are classified by polishing ratio and additives under National Tax Agency labelling rules.

Japan is an island country in East Asia whose drinks landscape is centred on sake, alongside shochu, whisky, beer and wine. Sake is made from rice, water, koji and yeast, with saccharification and fermentation running in parallel; shochu and whisky rely on distillation; beer and wine are shaped more by modern industry and imported drinking culture. Understanding Japanese drinks means looking at raw materials, process and labelling together.

Geography and climate

The Japanese archipelago runs from north to south, with climates from cool Hokkaido to subtropical Okinawa. Most of the country is influenced by monsoons and the ocean, with humid summers, cold winters and abundant rainfall. Sake regions are often where good water and rice are available, such as Niigata, Hyogo, Kyoto and the Tohoku region; wine regions are concentrated in Yamagata, Nagano and Hokkaido, where slopes and diurnal temperature ranges help.

Water and rice are the geographical foundation of sake. Soft water, hard water and mineral composition affect fermentation and flavour; sake rice (such as Yamada Nishiki) affects polishing and koji making through grain size, shinpaku and water absorption. Climate influences sake indirectly through rice quality and fermentation temperature control, rather than directly determining ripeness as in wine.

History

Sake brewing in Japan goes back to antiquity, with rice farming and fermentation techniques arriving from the continent and gradually becoming localised. In the Edo period, breweries and distribution networks developed, and modern science and temperature control were introduced later. In the later twentieth century, consumption patterns changed and ginjo styles and small local breweries gained attention. Shochu developed traditions in Kyushu and Okinawa, while the whisky industry was established in the twentieth century and has gained international attention in recent years.

Sake

Sake is made mainly from rice through polishing, washing, soaking, steaming, koji making, yeast starter, fermentation, pressing, filtration and pasteurisation. Saccharification and fermentation run in parallel: koji converts starch in steamed rice into sugar while yeast converts sugar into alcohol. This "multiple parallel fermentation" differs from beer and whisky, where saccharification precedes fermentation.

Key sake measures include polishing ratio, sake meter value, acidity and amino acidity. The polishing ratio is the proportion of the rice remaining after milling; a lower figure means more of the starchy centre is used. Saccharification and koji making affect the sugar composition and flavour. Sake "specific-name" categories are classified by polishing ratio and additives, such as junmai, ginjo and honjozo, with definitions set by National Tax Agency labelling rules.

Sake styles range from light and crisp to rich and sweet. Ginjo emphasises low-temperature fermentation and aroma, junmai emphasises rice flavour and body, and namazake and aged sake involve choices about pasteurisation and storage. Drinking temperatures range from chilled to warm, depending on the sake and personal preference.

Shochu and other spirits

Shochu is an important Japanese distilled spirit made from rice, barley, sweet potato, brown sugar and other raw materials, obtained by distillation. Kagoshima and Okinawa are major regions, and awamori is Okinawa's rice-based distilled spirit with its own process and regulations. Shochu alcohol content is usually lower than whisky, with styles from light to rich.

Japanese whisky was established as an industry in the twentieth century and has gained international attention in recent years through mizunara, sherry and other cask combinations. Yamazaki is one of the representative Japanese whiskies, showing how local water, climate and barrel ageing can shape a style. Oak ageing strongly affects whisky aroma and structure, and cask type and ageing environment are key to understanding Japanese whisky.

Beer and wine

Beer in Japan includes large industrial brands and small craft breweries. Craft beer emphasises hop aroma, malt recipes and local character, covering lager, ale, stout and more. The wine industry is small, with Yamagata, Nagano and Hokkaido as main regions; European varieties dominate, and styles are shaped by climate and winemaking technique.

Different drink categories have different regulations and labels. Sake and shochu are administered by the National Tax Agency, while beer and wine have their own tax and labelling requirements; sake's "specific-name" categories should not be applied to other drinks.

Regions and styles

  • Niigata: a core sake region, known for a light, dry style and good water.
  • Hyogo: an important region for Yamada Nishiki sake rice, with traditions in both sake and shochu.
  • Kyoto and the Kinki region: a deep sake tradition with varied styles.
  • Tohoku: a cool climate and rice region, with generally fresh sake styles.
  • Yamagata and Nagano: wine regions where slopes and diurnal range affect ripeness.
  • Kagoshima and Okinawa: core regions for shochu and awamori.

Regulation and labelling

Japanese alcohol labelling is administered by the National Tax Agency. Sake specific-name categories are classified by polishing ratio, additives and brewing method, and labels carry terms such as junmai, ginjo and honjozo. Sake and shochu also have labelling requirements for alcohol content, ingredients and production method.

When reading a label, distinguish brand, brewery, region, type and polishing ratio. "Junmai" means no added brewing alcohol, and "ginjo" means low-temperature fermentation and aroma; neither is a synonym for higher quality. Definitions should be checked against current regulations and brewery information.

Market and drinking

The domestic market is led by sake, beer and shochu, while whisky and wine have steady demand in restaurants and the gift market. Sake suits sushi, sashimi and light dishes; shochu suits meals and mixed drinks; whisky suits neat, with water or with ice; beer suits everyday and restaurant occasions.

Drinking should follow moderation. Sake is sensitive to temperature and light and should be finished soon after opening; whisky and shochu are more stable but should still be kept away from heat and direct light.

Common misconceptions

First, that sake is simply "rice wine". Its raw materials, process and labelling system differ from Chinese rice wine.

Second, that a lower polishing ratio is always better. Polishing ratio affects style and cost, not quality alone.

Third, that sake is always drunk warm. Different sake suit different temperatures, and chilled drinking is also common.

Fourth, that all Japanese whisky is imported spirit. Japan has domestic distillation and ageing, and imported spirit also exists in complex cases; the label is the guide.

Fifth, that shochu is the same as sake. Shochu is distilled and sake is fermented, with different processes and alcohol levels.
