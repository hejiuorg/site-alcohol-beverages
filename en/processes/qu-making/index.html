Qu making

A traditional process that cultures moulds, yeasts and bacteria on grain to make a saccharifying and fermenting starter; qu quality and microbial community directly affect yield and flavour in baijiu and yellow wine.

Qu making is a key step in traditional Chinese brewing: culturing microbes on grain to make a saccharifying and fermenting starter. Qu is not a single yeast but a carrier on which moulds, yeasts and bacteria grow together; it both breaks starch down into sugar and takes part in fermentation and aroma production. Baijiu, yellow wine and some other grain drinks depend on qu, but the qu types and processes differ.

Figure: Qu making. Image: Uranassbandit, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

What qu does

Qu has three core roles. First, it supplies amylases and glucoamylases that break grain starch into fermentable sugar. Second, it supplies yeasts that convert sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide. Third, it brings metabolites from bacteria and moulds that form acids, esters, alcohols and other flavour compounds.

Solid-state fermentation in Chinese baijiu often uses "simultaneous saccharification and fermentation", so qu quality directly affects yield and flavour. Qu is not only a fermentation starter but also a source of flavour. Understanding qu as simply "yeast powder" ignores its multiple roles in saccharification, acid production and aroma.

Main types

By raw material and use, common qu types include daqu, xiaoqu, fuqu and maiqiu. Daqu uses wheat, barley and peas, is pressed into bricks, and is classified by incubation temperature into low-, medium- and high-temperature types; xiaoqu uses rice flour or rice bran and is common in xiaoqu baijiu; fuqu uses bran as a carrier inoculated with pure moulds and gives higher yield and shorter cycles; maiqiu is used for yellow wine and is made from wheat.

Different qu types host different microbial communities, and flavour differences are obvious. Open-culture daqu brings more complex communities, while pure-culture fuqu is more controllable and efficient. The two are not simple substitutes but correspond to different product routes and production scales.

Production steps

The basic flow is: grinding raw materials, adding water and mixing, treading or pressing into shape, incubating in the qu room, turning the qu, removing it from the room and storing it. Mixing moisture, brick tightness, room temperature and humidity, and the timing of turning are all critical control points.

During incubation, microbes multiply and generate heat, and manual turning releases heat and renews air; after removal, the qu is stored for a period so that its activity stabilises. Every step affects the final microbial structure, so qu making is both a technical operation and an accumulation of experience.

Key controls

Temperature is one of the most important variables. Low-temperature qu incubates cooler, with moulds and yeasts dominating and a cleaner flavour; high-temperature qu is hotter, with more heat-tolerant bacteria and often richer roast, nut and sauce-like notes.

Too much moisture invites contamination, while too little limits microbial growth. Turning timing affects oxygen distribution and heat release in the brick, and turning too early or too late changes the community structure. Freshness of raw materials, hygiene in the qu room and ventilation also affect how contaminants compete.

Influence on flavour

Qu sets the microbial starting point of the fermenting mash. Moulds in daqu provide saccharifying capacity, yeasts handle alcoholic fermentation, and bacteria contribute acid and ester precursors. Different qu types, seasons and room management can make the same raw material produce different styles.

The medium-temperature qu of strong-aroma baijiu, the high-temperature qu of sauce-aroma baijiu and the low-temperature qu of light-aroma baijiu are important clues for understanding style differences; Maotai and Fenjiu represent the high-temperature and low-temperature routes respectively. This describes the match between process and raw material, not a quality ranking of specific brands.

Storage and use

After leaving the qu room, qu is usually stored for a period so that microbial and enzyme activity stabilises and contamination risk falls. The storage environment should be dry, ventilated and dark, protected from moisture and insects.

When used, qu is ground to a suitable particle size and matched with raw material, water and temperature; dosage, grind size and mixing evenness all affect saccharification and fermentation. Seasonal changes in temperature and humidity also require adjustments to storage and use. Poor storage reduces qu activity and affects yield and flavour stability.

Qu making and seasons

Traditional qu making is closely tied to season. Temperature and humidity affect microbial growth in the qu room, and producers often adjust incubation time, turning frequency and brick thickness according to the season. Sayings such as "making qu at Dragon Boat Festival and adding grain at Double Ninth" reflect sauce-aroma baijiu's dependence on seasonal rhythm, though specific practice still varies by distillery, region and year.

Understanding seasonal factors helps explain why the same qu type can differ when produced at different times. Qu making is therefore not a fixed recipe but an experience-based craft adjusted to the environment; qu from the same distillery in different years may also differ because of climate and raw materials. This is also why traditional qu making relies heavily on experience.

Common misconceptions

First, more qu is better. Dosage must match raw material, process and fermentation cycle; excess may bring bitterness or uncontrolled fermentation.

Second, qu is just yeast. Qu is a mixed microbial system containing moulds, yeasts and bacteria, with functions far beyond alcoholic fermentation.

Third, mechanical qu is always better than traditional qu. Pure-culture fuqu is efficient but usually less complex in flavour than traditional daqu, and the two suit different product routes.

Fourth, qu making is just "mouldy grain". Temperature, moisture, turning and storage together determine the microbial structure; surface appearance alone is not enough.

Fifth, traditional qu is always safer than mechanical qu. Safety depends on raw materials, hygiene management and testing, not on the label "traditional" or "modern" itself.

Start with the inputs. From there, consider the sequence of steps, then what changes at the end. That is how a practical judgment about Qu making is built.
