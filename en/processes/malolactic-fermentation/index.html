Malolactic fermentation

A secondary fermentation in which lactic acid bacteria convert malic acid into lactic acid and carbon dioxide; it lowers titratable acidity, raises pH and softens texture, adding creamy notes in moderation but volatile acidity, biogenic amines and haze when uncontrolled.

Malolactic fermentation, shortened to MLF, is a secondary fermentation in wine. It is often confused with alcoholic fermentation, but the agents differ completely: alcoholic fermentation uses yeast to turn sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide, while malolactic fermentation uses lactic acid bacteria to turn malic acid into lactic acid and carbon dioxide and produces no alcohol. The two can run in sequence or be co-inoculated towards the end of alcoholic fermentation.

Figure: Malolactic fermentation. Image: Agne27, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Place in the process

MLF follows alcoholic fermentation or overlaps its final stage. It is a deacidification and stabilisation treatment rather than a drink category of its own. It takes place in the wine, the lees and the microbial community on vessel surfaces, and only afterwards does the wine move to sulfur dioxide adjustment, clarification and ageing. For red wine it is almost routine; for white wine and sparkling base wine it depends on target acidity and style. Calling it a synonym for every "secondary fermentation" confuses it with in-bottle sparkling fermentation.

Steps and parameters

A common practice is to inoculate a selected lactic acid bacterium after alcoholic fermentation, hold 18–22 °C and allow 2–4 weeks; pH should be 3.3–3.5, since most strains struggle to start below 3.2; free sulfur dioxide usually needs to be below 10–15 mg/L, because too much molecular sulfur dioxide blocks the bacteria outright. These ranges follow the common descriptions in the OIV material, wine reference work and AWRI material listed on this process. Some producers co-inoculate late in alcoholic fermentation to shorten the total cycle and reduce competition for nutrients. Completion is judged by residual malic acid below 0.1–0.2 g/L.

Mechanism: malolactic enzyme and citrate metabolism

The malolactic enzyme of lactic acid bacteria decarboxylates L-malic acid into L-lactic acid and carbon dioxide and consumes one proton, so titratable acidity falls and pH rises, usually by 0.1–0.3 units. Malic acid is diprotic and lactic acid monoprotic, so the acid sensation shifts from sharp to soft. Some strains also metabolise citrate into diacetyl, acetoin and 2,3-butanediol, and diacetyl brings buttery, creamy notes. Heterofermentative strains can convert sugar into lactate, acetate, ethanol and carbon dioxide, which raises volatile acidity.

Equipment and variants

MLF needs no special equipment beyond temperature-controlled tanks, sampling valves and sulfur dioxide measurement. Variants include spontaneous fermentation, inoculation with selected strains, co-inoculation late in alcoholic fermentation, and stopping at a chosen point by membrane filtration or centrifugation. Some producers work in oak so that micro-oxygenation and bacterial activity happen together; others add bacterial nutrients and adjust pH and temperature to improve success. Vessel shape affects temperature gradients and lees suspension, and therefore how evenly the fermentation proceeds.

Failure modes and diagnosis

First, MLF that will not start: malic acid stays high, usually because pH is too low, temperature too low, free sulfur dioxide too high or nutrition is poor. Second, rising volatile acidity, smelling of vinegar or nail polish remover, from heterofermentative lactobacilli or acetic acid bacteria. Third, biogenic amine accumulation, especially histamine and tyramine, linked to long lees contact and wild microbial activity. Fourth, haze or viscosity from strains that produce extracellular polysaccharides. Diagnosis should combine enzymatic malic acid assay, volatile acidity, pH and sensory checks rather than waiting for visible bubbling.

Effect on flavour

The most direct change is acid structure: as malic acid falls, the wine feels rounder and softer, and red wine tannin becomes easier to accept. Moderate diacetyl gives creamy, buttery and bready notes, common in some Chardonnay; in excess it makes the wine dull and margarine-like. In red wine, MLF supports stability and softness; in the base wine for sparkling wine, producers often avoid it deliberately to keep acidity and fruit, unless a rounder style is the goal.

Effect on stability

By consuming malic acid that bacteria could otherwise use, MLF lowers the risk of refermentation in bottle, which is a key part of red wine stability. The rise in pH weakens the antimicrobial efficiency of sulfur dioxide, so free and molecular sulfur dioxide must be recalculated afterwards. If wine is bottled before MLF is complete, residual malic acid can be used by lactic acid bacteria in bottle, producing haze, pressure and off-flavours. Once biogenic amines form they cannot be removed by filtration, so control depends on strain choice and hygiene.

Common misconceptions

First, treating MLF as part of alcoholic fermentation and ignoring that bacteria do the work. Second, assuming every red wine must undergo MLF, when high-acid varieties and some fresh styles may skip it. Third, reading a buttery note as a mark of quality, when excess diacetyl is a fault. Fourth, believing inoculation guarantees success, when pH, temperature and sulfur dioxide can still stall the process. Fifth, confusing in-bottle sparkling fermentation with MLF: the former is driven by yeast and produces alcohol and bubbles.

Malolactic fermentation is carried out by lactic acid bacteria, which convert sharper malic acid into softer lactic acid and carbon dioxide. It lowers titratable acidity, raises pH and can add creamy, buttery notes. It is not a second alcoholic fermentation, and it is not compulsory for every red wine. If it starts late or runs uncontrolled, volatile acidity and off-flavours can follow.
